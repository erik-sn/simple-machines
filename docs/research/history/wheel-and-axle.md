# The wheel and axle

Not the wheel that rolls under a cart, but the wheel that turns a smaller axle: the windlass, the capstan, the doorknob, the steering wheel. Galileo called it "a perpetual lever," a lever that never runs out of travel, and chained together as toothed wheels it becomes the gear train by which, on paper, five talents move a thousand.

Reliability tags: [P] primary source seen in text or translation, [S] scholarly secondary, [W] Wikipedia-level or museum-level, [U] unverified or recalled, check before use.

## Physics in plain terms

- A wheel fixed to a smaller axle so the two turn together. Effort applied at the wheel's rim, load taken at the axle: ideal mechanical advantage = wheel radius / axle radius. Reverse the roles (effort at the axle, as in a driven cart wheel) and the advantage is less than 1: force is traded for speed and distance. [W]
- It is a lever with the fulcrum at the axle's centre, and a lever that never runs out of travel. Galileo, Le Mecaniche, "Dell'asse nella ruota e dell'argano": the two instruments "dependono immediatamente dalla lieva, anzi non sono altro che un vette perpetuo" (depend immediately on the lever, indeed are nothing but a perpetual lever); "la forza al peso aver sempre l'istessa proporzione, che il semidiametro dell'asse al semidiametro della ruota" (force is to weight as the semidiameter of the axle to the semidiameter of the wheel). [P]
- The trade: in his ten-to-one example the load "non si sarà mosso più che la decima parte di quello che averà caminato il movente" (will have moved no more than a tenth of what the mover has walked). [P]
- Guidobaldo, De axe in peritrochio (1577), proposition 1 (Pigafetta's Italian, 1581, p. 103): "La possanza sostenente il peso con l'asse nella rota, ha la proportione medesima al peso, che il mezo diametro dell'asse al mezo diametro della rota" (the power sustaining the weight has the same proportion to the weight as the semidiameter of the axle to the semidiameter of the wheel), with the corollary "la possanza sempre è minore del peso" (the power is always less than the weight), since the axle is always smaller than the wheel. [P: archive.org lemechaniche00mont]
- Windlass and capstan: a windlass has a horizontal axle, a capstan a vertical one, walked round by men on bars. Vitruvius already distinguishes the sucula (windlass) from the ergata (capstan). [P; W]
- Gear trains are wheel-and-axles in series; the advantages multiply. Wilkins's chapter title of 1648 puts it well: "Of the Wheel, by multiplication of which it is easie to move any imaginable weight." [P]
- Friction: the axle bearing is where a wheel-and-axle loses, and the loss is small because the rubbing surface is at the small radius. Leonardo measured sliding friction in the 1490s (proportional to load, independent of area, coefficient about a quarter) and drew caged ball bearings (Codex Madrid I, f. 20v); Amontons restated the laws in 1699. [W; S: Hutchings 2016, U for its details]

## Earliest evidence

- The rolling wheel. The Bronocice pot from Poland (Funnelbeaker culture, found 1975) bears an incised wagon; its pit is radiocarbon dated 3637-3373 BC at two sigma, median 3520 BC. Proto-cuneiform tablets from Uruk show sledge-on-wheels signs, dated c. 3500-3350 BC by some and to the end of the 4th millennium by others. The Ljubljana Marshes wheel (Slovenia, found 2002) is ash with an oak axle, about 70 cm across, dated 3340-3030 BC; its square axle hole means the wheel and axle turned together, a true wheel-and-axle pair. Evidence appears near-simultaneously in the Caucasus, the Pontic steppe and the Cucuteni-Trypillia culture; cart tracks at Flintbek, Germany, c. 3400 BC. [S: Bronocice paper in apolona; W]
- Spoked wheels and chariots: Sintashta graves in the southern Urals, with a Bayesian date of 2050-1900 BC for the earliest chariots, "no later than the first proto-chariots of the ancient Near East" (Lindner, Antiquity 2020). [S]
- The potter's wheel: slow turntables in the Near East by c. 3500 BC (some pages say 4500); the fast flywheel wheel in the mid to late 3rd millennium; a Sumerian stone wheel from Ur c. 3129 BC; a claim for Cucuteni-Trypillia in the 5th millennium. Wikipedia's own verdict: "the exact origin of the wheel is not wholly clear yet." Pseudo-Aristotle counts the potter's wheel as the third kind of circular motion, turning "parallel to the ground." [W; P]
- The bow drill: jasper bits at Mehrgarh, Pakistan, in the 5th to 4th millennium BC, drilling lapis and carnelian. [W]
- The windlass: Wikipedia's claim that the oldest description is in Isidore of Seville (7th century) ignores Vitruvius and Heron; treat as doubtful. The earliest picture of a well windlass is in Wang Zhen's Nong shu (1313); a surviving medieval windlass of 1360-1400 is in Chesterfield church. [W, doubtful]
- Assyrian reliefs of Sennacherib (700-692 BC) show colossi on sledges over rollers, hauled by rope with men on long levers, and water lifted by counterpoised bucket arms: rollers and levers, not windlasses. [W: British Museum]

## Antiquity

- Pseudo-Aristotle, Mechanical Problems (c. 300 BC). Problem 8: circles move easily and turn in three ways, "along its circumference ... as a carriage wheel revolves; or round the centre only, as pulleys move ... or it can turn, as does the potter's wheel, parallel to the ground." Problem 13, the windlass: "Why is it that longer bars are moved more easily than shorter ones round the same capstan, and similarly lighter windlasses are moved more easily by the same force than stouter windlasses? Is it because the windlass and the capstan form a centre and the outer masses the radii? For the radii of greater circles are moved more readily and further by the same force." Problem 24, the wheel of Aristotle: why a large circle and a small one about the same centre trace equal paths when rolled together, a puzzle that Cardano, Mersenne and Galileo (Two New Sciences, with concentric hexagons) all worried at. [P: Forster's Oxford translation]
- Archimedes: Heron cites his lost On Balances for the proof that "greater circles overpower smaller when their movement is about the same centre" (Heath, citing Pappus VIII p. 1068). The tradition that Archimedes invented the windlass is tradition only. [S: Heath II; W]
- Heron, Mechanics (1st century AD). The first of the five powers is the axle-in-wheel, in Latin axis in peritrochio, the name the Renaissance kept. Book II describes its construction, a wheel fitted on an axle with bronze rings, then its theory; the book ends with three ways to move "a weight of 1000 talents by means of a force of 5 talents": the toothed-wheel train "described in the Baroulkos," a pulley system, and combined levers. The baroulkos (Book I.1; also Dioptra 37) is a box of meshing toothed wheels with an endless screw and crank at the input; the Kotsanas museum's reconstruction uses a gear ratio of 1:5:5:5:8/5 to reduce the pull to a two-hundredth, and notes that in practice more reduction is needed to beat friction. Drachmann treated it as a paper demonstration of the principle rather than a working machine (recollection). [S: Heath II pp. 346-352; W: Kotsanas; U for Drachmann's wording]
- Vitruvius X.2 (c. 30-20 BC): the windlass, "close to each end of the windlass are two holes, so adjusted that handspikes can be fitted into them," and "the rope winds round the windlass, gets taut, and thus raises the load"; for heavier loads an axle "carrying on its centre a large drum, which some term a wheel, but the Greeks call it amphiesis or perithekion," turned by a capstan (ergata); "but if a larger drum is placed either in the middle or at one side, without any capstan, men can tread in it and accomplish the work more expeditiously," the treadwheel. Vitruvius gives proportions, not sizes. [P: Morgan; Latin on Perseus]
- Vitruvius X.2.11-15 tells the rolling-wheel stories: Chersiphron moved the column shafts of the temple of Artemis at Ephesus by fitting iron pivots into their ends and rolling them inside a frame; his son Metagenes rolled the architraves inside twelve-foot wheels; and the contractor Paconius tried to roll a pedestal block inside fifteen-foot wheels wound with rope, but it "kept swerving out to one side" and he went bankrupt. These are rolling tricks, not the machine of this file, but they are the best Roman wheel story. [P]
- Roman treadwheel cranes: the polyspastos "worked by four men at both sides of the winch, could lift 3000 kg"; with a treadwheel "the maximum load even doubled to 6000 kg at only half the crew" (O'Connor via Wikipedia). The Haterii relief (Vatican, Museo Gregoriano Profano, cat. 9997-9998; found on the Via Labicana in 1848) shows "a sort of crane which is powered by a large wheel worked by slaves" beside the monuments the family had built, dated late 1st century by most art historians and early 2nd by the Vatican. [W]
- Water-lifting wheels: at Rio Tinto sixteen wheels in eight pairs, each pair lifting about 3.5 m for 30 m in all, trodden by men on slats at the rim, their parts numbered for assembly; a fragment in the British Museum (about 4.6 m across); the Dolaucothi wheel fragment carbon-dated to about AD 90 (Boon and Williams, JRS 1966). [W; S]
- Gearing: Vitruvius X.5 is the first technical description of a geared watermill (c. 40-10 BC); Barbegal (2nd century) ran sixteen wheels; the Hierapolis sawmill (3rd century) is "the earliest known machine to incorporate the mechanism of a crank and connecting rod." The Antikythera mechanism (c. 150-100 BC, proposals from 205 to 60 BC; at least 30 surviving gears) shows that Hellenistic gear-cutting was far ahead of anything a crane needed. [W; S: Freeth]

## Medieval

- Treadwheel cranes reappear in France about 1225 (magna rota), in a manuscript of 1240, and in harbours at Utrecht (1244), Antwerp (1263), Bruges (1288) and Hamburg (1291); in England by 1331 (Matthies, Technology and Culture 1992). [W citing S]
- Gdansk's Zuraw: first mentioned 1367, rebuilt 1442-1444 after a fire, brick towers 1483; two pairs of wooden wheels about 6 m across, walked from inside; lifted about 2 tons to 27 m or 4 tons to 11 m with the wheels coupled, and stepped ships' masts; doubled as a city gate; four fifths destroyed in 1945 and rebuilt. Figures differ between sources. [W]
- Spinning wheel: origin disputed between India (500-1000), Zhou China and the Middle East by the 11th century; the earliest clear picture is from Baghdad in 1237 (al-Hariri's Maqamat), China c. 1270, Europe c. 1280; the treadle is attributed to Brunswick in 1533; Leonardo sketched the flyer. [W]
- Windmills: Persian vertical-axis mills in the 8th-9th centuries; the first certain northern European reference is Weedley, Yorkshire, 1185. Watermills: Domesday (1086) counts 5,624 in England. Both turn the wheel-and-axle into a power train through gears. [W]
- The crank: a crank-turned grindstone in the Utrecht Psalter (c. 830); two cranks on a grindstone in the Luttrell Psalter (c. 1340); the connecting rod "reappeared" with compound cranks and flywheels in the Anonymous of the Hussite Wars (c. 1430); al-Jazari's twin-cylinder pump (1206) has a crankshaft. [W]
- Islamic engineering: the Banu Musa (c. 850) use cranks that approach a crankshaft and conical valves as the first automatic controllers; al-Muradi (11th century al-Andalus, manuscript of 1266) builds geared clocks with segmental and epicyclic gearing driven by water wheels, which Hill called "the first geared clock"; al-Jazari (1206) gives fifty devices with five water-raising machines, the crankshaft chain pump, segmental gears, laminated timber and statically balanced wheels. [W; S: Hill]
- The crossbow windlass: spanning belts 1200-1350, goat's foot 1300-1500, windlass 1350-1550, cranequin 1400-1550 (Tod's Workshop); a windlass crossbow gives an advantage near 160:1 and spans bows of 1250 lb draw. Wikipedia's "1215" for the first windlass spanner is earlier than most; the cranequin is a rack and pinion. [W; dates vary]

## Renaissance

- Leonardo, Codex Madrid I (1490s): the first part is "sketches and notes on elementary mechanisms, gears, levers, springs, bearings, and chains," the treatise on machine elements Leonardo meant to write; worm gears that engage several teeth (often cited as f. 17v, [U]), caged ball bearings (f. 20v), friction studies from 1493. [W: Museo Leonardiano; Codex Madrid]
- Francesco di Giorgio Martini, Trattato (after 1482), Opusculum de architectura (c. 1475) and the Vatican Codicetto: mills, pile drivers, hauling machines, winches, cranes and pumps, a stock the later theatres of machines drew on. [W]
- Agricola, De re metallica (1556), Book VI, "Hauling machines are of varied and diverse forms ... and if I am not mistaken, they were unknown to the Ancients." Five dry-load machines: a windlass with a barrel and two cranks, "two robust men turn the windlass, each having a wheelbarrow near him" (illustration 161); a windlass with a flywheel, "sometimes masses of lead are hung on to this wheel ... in order that when it is turned they depress the spokes by their weight and increase the motion" (162); a tread whim with an upright axle and plank wheel trodden by two men driving a toothed drum and lantern, serving "even to a depth of 180 feet" (163); a horse whim in a conical shed fifty feet across with a forty-foot axle, "two horses ... if ... the shaft is very deep four horses" (165); and a geared horse whim with a brake for a shaft "almost two hundred and forty feet deep" (167). A rag-and-chain pump is driven by a treadwheel "twenty-three feet high and four feet wide" trodden by two men, lifting from 66 feet (197); a horse treadmill drives bellows for ventilation. [P: Hoover translation, Gutenberg 38015]
- Besson, Theatrum instrumentorum (1578): lathes, stone cutters, saw mills, hauling machines, cranes, elevators, pumps. [W]
- Ramelli (1588): over a hundred of the 195 plates are water-raising machines driven by wheels, cranks and gear trains; 21 are grain mills; plate 188 is the bookwheel, six feet across, with "spur gears and an epicyclic gear train" borrowed "from astronomical clocks," so that a reader "can see and turn through a large number of books without moving from one spot," recommended for those "tormented by gout." [W; S: Science History Institute]
- Zonca, Novo teatro (1607): 42 plates including the water-driven silk-throwing mill, a printing press and an endless screw and winch; plates redrawn in the Chinese Qiqi tushuo of 1627. The story that Zonca's printed plate gave away the Piedmontese silk-mill secret that John Lombe later copied for Derby (1717-21) is common but unverified here. [W; U]
- Guidobaldo (1577), De axe in peritrochio, two propositions, reduces the machine to the lever; Galileo (c. 1600) does the same and adds the capstan: an axle "di figura colonnare, volubile intorno al centro B" with the rope wound on it and a bar at whose end "la forza di un uomo, o vero di un cavallo, o di altro animale atto nato a tirare, il quale, movendosi in giro, camini sopra la circonferenza del cerchio" (the force of a man, or a horse, or another animal born to pull, which moving in a circle walks on the circumference). [P]
- Wilkins, Mathematicall Magick (1648), chapter XII: "The Wheel or axis in peritrochio ... if we conceive the same difference betwixt the Semidiameter of the wheels or spokes AC, and the Semidiameter of the axis AB, as there is betwixt the weight of the world and the strength of a man," a man could move the world. Archimedes' boast, restated for the wheel. [P]
- Hutton's Mathematical and Philosophical Dictionary (1795-96): "These simple machines are usually accounted six in number, viz, the Lever, the Wheel and Axle, or Axis in Peritrochio, the Pulley, the Inclined Plane, the Wedge, and the Screw." The canonical six, in print, by the end of the 18th century. [P]

## The key insight and who had it

- Pseudo-Aristotle (c. 300 BC): longer bars and bigger wheels move more easily because "the radii of greater circles are moved more readily and further by the same force."
- Heron (1st century AD): the axle-in-wheel is the first power, and chained as toothed wheels it moves any weight with any force, on paper.
- Guidobaldo (1577) and Galileo (c. 1600): the lever with the fulcrum at the axle, "a perpetual lever," force to weight as axle radius to wheel radius, distance traded the other way.
- Leonardo (1490s): friction, bearings and the machine elements that decide whether a real wheel-and-axle works.

## Story beats

- A family of Roman contractors carves their own treadwheel crane, slaves inside the wheel, on their tomb beside the buildings they raised. [W]
- Paconius rolls a pedestal block inside two great wheels wound with rope; it keeps swerving off the road, and he goes bankrupt. Chersiphron, a century earlier, had rolled the Ephesus columns on iron pivots and got them there. [P: Vitruvius X.2]
- Gdansk: men walking inside two six-metre wheels for four centuries, stepping ships' masts, in a crane that is also the city gate. [W]
- Agricola's miners hang lumps of lead on the spokes of a windlass to make a flywheel. [P]
- Ramelli's bookwheel: a Renaissance reading machine on astronomical-clock gearing, for the gouty scholar. [W]
- Capstan shanties: "Shenandoah" and "Rio Grande" were sung to keep the feet in step while walking the anchor up. [W: Britannica, page not fetched]

## Descendants

- Doorknob (knob handles on 1st-century doors from Lake Nemi; over a hundred US patents 1830-1876), screwdriver handle, faucet handle, steering wheel (Alfred Vacheron on a Panhard for the 1894 Paris-Rouen race; ship's wheels from the early 18th century), winch and capstan, fishing reel (a 1195 Chinese painting; Barker's Art of Angling, 1651, "a winde, to turne with a barrell"), bicycle cranks, hand drill and brace, the crank on every engine, gear trains, turbines. [W]
- Belt drives and the "pulleys" of a car engine are wheel-and-axles transmitting rotation, not the force-dividing pulley of pulley.md.

## Misconceptions to avoid

- "The cart wheel is the simple machine." The rolling wheel reduces friction and, driven at the axle, multiplies speed; the wheel-and-axle machine is the windlass and the doorknob. The Ljubljana wheel, with its square axle hole, is both.
- "The wheel reduces work." It trades force for distance like every other machine.
- "The Egyptians used the wheel and axle to build the pyramids." Rollers, sledges and levers; no windlass evidence.
- "Isidore of Seville first described the windlass." Vitruvius and Heron did, six centuries earlier.
- "The ancients had no hauling machines." Agricola thought so; Vitruvius proves otherwise.
- "The Antikythera mechanism is a simple machine." It is a gear train computing the heavens; cite it for gear-cutting, not for lifting.

## Numbers for the illustrator and the physics stage

- A well windlass with a 40 cm crank and a 5 cm drum: advantage 8; a 10 kgf turn lifts an 80 kg bucket, and the hand travels 8 m of circle per metre of rope. [derived]
- A capstan with 2 m bars and a 30 cm barrel: advantage about 13 per bar; four men at 40 kgf each pull about 2 tonnes on the rope, walking 13 m for every metre of rope. [derived]
- A treadwheel 4 m across on a 40 cm drum: advantage 10; two men of 70 kg walking the wheel can hold about 1.4 tonnes on the rope, which is why Vitruvius's treadwheel doubled the polyspastos's capacity at half the crew. [derived; S for the Vitruvian figures]
- Heron's baroulkos: 1000 talents with 5, a ratio of 200; the Kotsanas train 5 x 5 x 5 x 8/5 = 200 exactly. [S]
- A doorknob 6 cm across on a 1 cm spindle: advantage 6, which is why a knob turns a latch that a bare spindle will not. [derived]
