# Compound machines

A compound machine is a chain of simple machines in which the output of one is the input of the next. The advantages multiply and so do the losses. Heron's book already treats "combinations of these powers"; the whole point of his baroulkos is that five talents can move a thousand if enough wheels are put between them. The Renaissance theatres of machines are, plate after plate, compound machines drawn without their theory; Guidobaldo and Galileo supplied the theory one machine at a time and left the reader to multiply.

Reliability tags: [P] primary source seen in text or translation, [S] scholarly secondary, [W] Wikipedia-level or museum-level, [U] unverified or recalled, [derived] a calculation done for this dossier.

## How advantages combine

- In series, ideal advantages multiply: a lever of 4 driving a tackle of 3 gives 12. So do the distance ratios: the effort travels 12 times as far as the load. [textbook]
- Efficiencies multiply too. Three stages at 80 percent each deliver 51 percent. This is why very long trains stall on friction, and why Heron's paper machine of 1000 talents by 5 would not turn in bronze without the extra reduction the Kotsanas reconstruction notes. [W: Kotsanas; derived]
- A machine can be compound in space (a capstan, whose bar is a lever and whose barrel is an axle) or in time (a wedge that is struck, ratcheting forward blow by blow). Percussion is the one input the Renaissance could not fit into the multiplication, which is why Galileo ended Le Mecaniche with a chapter on it and never finished the theory. [P; S]
- The bookkeeping is the same as for one machine: input force times input path equals output force times output path, less what friction takes at every joint. Lagrange's principle of virtual velocities (1788) says exactly this for any number of stages at once. [P: Lagrange]

## Ancient compound machines

- Heron's baroulkos (Mechanics I.1; Dioptra 37; Pappus VIII proposition 10). A box of meshing toothed wheels, each pair with a 5:1 ratio, ending in an endless screw turned by a crank: "the force operating at the crank is even more effective, for the crank describes a circle greater than the perimeter of the screw." The worked example is 1000 talents moved by 5. Heron then shows the same task done with pulleys and with combined levers, and says of the whole class that "just as on a balance, the force will be in equilibrium with the weight." Pappus uses the passage to introduce "give me where I may stand, and I move the earth," so the boast is attached, in its earliest source, to a gear train rather than a lever. [P via Cohen and Drabkin and Hultsch; S: Heath II; Schiefsky 2008]
- Heron on the illusion of free work, Mechanics I.20 (Cohen and Drabkin): "Many people have the erroneous belief that weights placed on the ground may be moved only by forces equivalent to these weights." He then explains rollers, friction and the plane. [P via roger-pearse]
- Archimedes' ship. Plutarch's demonstration is a "system of compound pulleys" (polyspaston) worked by hand; a plausible reconstruction is a winch feeding a multi-sheave tackle, that is, an axle-in-wheel driving a pulley. Athenaeus's launch of the Syracusia used a helix, a winding device or a screw. Proclus adds Hiero's verdict: "From that day forth Archimedes was to be believed in everything that he might say." [P: Plutarch; Athenaeus; S via Assis for Proclus]
- The claw of Archimedes (siege of Syracuse, 213-212 BC). Polybius: a beam over the wall let down "an iron hand attached to a chain," gripped the prow, and "the man who piloted the beam would press down the opposite end"; the prow lifted, the beam was made fast, and "by means of a rope and pulley" the hand was let drop. Livy adds the lead counterweight (gravi libramento plumbum). Rorres and Harris reconstruct it as a counterweighted lever with a release, not a pulley crane. A lever, a counterweight and a pulley release: a compound machine on a wall. [P: Paton; Moore; S: Rorres and Harris 2001]
- Vitruvius's cranes (X.2): a lever-shaped pair of timbers, a block of sheaves, a windlass worked by handspikes (a lever turning an axle feeding a tackle); the larger version adds a drum for a capstan or a treadwheel. Trispastos, pentaspastos, polyspastos: the names count sheaves, the theory counts strands. Lancaster's estimates from Vitruvius: one man on a trispastos, about 150 kg; four men on a winch with a polyspastos, about 3000 kg; a treadwheel doubles that at half the crew. [P: Morgan; S via W]
- The Roman treadwheel crane on the Haterii relief (Vatican, c. AD 100): treadwheel (axle-in-wheel) driving a tackle on a jib. [W]
- The Hierapolis sawmill (3rd century AD): water wheel, gear train, crank and connecting rod, the earliest known, driving two frame saws through marble. [W]
- Heron's Book II closes with three ways to move 1000 talents with 5 and a section on "combinations" (II.21-32) before the questions; the Greek programme was always about combining. [S: Schiefsky]

## Medieval compound machines

- The treadwheel crane returns (France c. 1225; Utrecht 1244; Bruges 1288; Gdansk 1367, rebuilt 1444): two men in a wheel lifting two to four tons through a tackle. Gdansk's paired six-metre wheels stepped ships' masts for four centuries. [W]
- Mills: a water wheel or wind sail (axle-in-wheel taking power from a stream) through a gear train (chained wheel-and-axles) to a millstone, a stamp, a saw or a bellows. Vitruvius X.5 gives the first geared watermill (c. 40-10 BC); Domesday counts 5,624 English watermills in 1086; the windmill is in Yorkshire by 1185. [W]
- The crossbow windlass (from about 1350): a crank on a drum feeding two small tackles, advantage near 160:1, to span a bow of 1250 lb. The cranequin (rack and pinion) is a wheel-and-axle on a lever-shaped rack. [W; Tod's Workshop]
- Al-Jazari's water-raising machines (1206): an animal-driven wheel through gearing to a crank and connecting rod driving pistons, or to a chain of pots; his castle clock chains a float, a pulley, a counterweight and segmental gears. Al-Muradi's clocks (11th century) run water wheels through segmental and epicyclic gearing to automata. [W; S: Hill]
- Villard de Honnecourt's water-powered saw (c. 1230): wheel, cam and spring pole, a compound machine that cuts and resets itself. [W]

## Renaissance compound machines

- Brunelleschi's ox hoist (Florence, c. 1420). Oxen on a whim (lever on an axle) drive a screw and two crown wheels that let the driver reverse the load without unhitching the beasts, with three drums of different diameter for three speeds, feeding a rope to the dome. Some 37,000 tons went up over sixteen years. Leonardo drew it (Codex Atlanticus f. 1083v); Taccola and Ghiberti recorded it; a separate revolving crane, the castello, worked on the dome itself. [W: Museo Leonardiano; Florence Cathedral; U for Prager and Scaglia's details]
- Taccola (De ingeneis 1433, De machinis 1449) and Francesco di Giorgio (Trattato, after 1482): column-raising machines with screw jacks and winches, cranes, mills, pile drivers, pumps. Screw jacks are a screw inside a lever (the handle) lifting through a nut; Francesco's column lifters chain jacks and winches. [W; U for specific plates]
- Leonardo, Codex Madrid I (1490s): the "elements of machines," gears, worms, ratchets, springs, bearings, chains, screws, and a 33-sheave block (f. 36v) for clocks. His worm-screw jack (f. 26r) is a screw driven by a worm gear driven by a crank: three stages in one box. [W: Museo Leonardiano]
- Agricola, De re metallica (1556), Book VI: windlasses with cranks and flywheels; tread whims with toothed drum and lantern; horse whims with forty-foot axles and gearing and a brake for shafts 240 feet deep; a treadwheel driving a rag-and-chain pump from 66 feet; horse treadmills driving bellows. Every one is an axle-in-wheel through gears to a rope or chain. [P: Hoover translation]
- Besson (1578): screw-cutting and swash-plate lathes (plates 7-9), cranes, presses, dredgers. [S: Dibner Library]
- Ramelli (1588): 110 water-raising machines (wheels, cranks, gear trains, pumps, chains), 21 grain mills, 10 cranes, 14 screw jacks, 7 dragging machines, 15 military bridges. Plate 156: a screw jack with worm and worm gear to lift a door off its hinges "operated by a single person, easily and with little noise"; plates 162-167: screw jacks for breaking bolts and bending gratings; plate 169: a crane with spur gear, lantern and lewis; plate 181: a weight-mover with crank, spur gear and lantern, worm and worm gear, its pulleys drawn enlarged in the background; plate 188: the bookwheel, epicyclic gearing keeping the shelves level. [S: Smithsonian; Science History Institute]
- Zonca (1607): the silk-throwing mill, a water wheel driving hundreds of spindles through gearing, the most complex machine drawn in the genre; the paper stampers; the printing press (lever driving a screw driving a platen). [W]
- The screw press itself, from Heron's presses through Gutenberg's, is a compound machine: a bar (lever) turns a screw (wound plane) that drives a platen. Gutenberg's addition was the bar. [W]

## What the Renaissance authors said about combining machines

- Heron and Pappus set the programme: to move a given weight with a given force, and to reduce all five powers "to one nature." Pappus (in Commandino's Latin): "Heron and Philon taught why these five powers are reduced to one nature, though very different in shape." Guidobaldo's preface says he follows Pappus "as leader," that Pappus "philosophised excellently about the five principal machines, the lever, pulley, axle in wheel, wedge and screw," and that everything in machines "must be referred to those five machines which are endowed with infinite force." [S: Palmieri 2008; Laird 2013]
- Guidobaldo (1577) on why the compound machine cannot be trusted to theory: a machine in equilibrium follows the mathematics exactly; disturb it and "all the irregularities and uncertainties of matter" enter. To Giacomo Contarini (1580): "the resistance that the material makes is made when weights are to be moved and not when they are merely to be sustained, because then the machine neither moves nor turns." For sustaining a weight there is one determinate power; the moving powers "may be infinite" and "one cannot give a firm rule for them." De trochlea, proposition 28, corollary 2: "the more easily a power can move a weight, the more slowly it does so." [S: Laird 2013]
- Pigafetta's note in the 1581 Italian, on pulleys: "Ma la esperientia poi molte volte mostra diversità, e si troviamo ingannati, facendo la materia grandemente variare le cose" (but experience then often shows differences, and we find ourselves deceived, the material making things vary greatly). The gap between drawn machine and built machine, stated by the translator. [P: archive.org lemechaniche00mont]
- Galileo, Le Mecaniche (c. 1600), opening chapter: the mechanicians who "deceive themselves" thinking that machines let a small force move a great weight; the four things to consider, weight, force, distance and time; "whoever hopes and tries by means of machines to get the same effect without adding slowness to the moved body will certainly be deceived" ("Ma chi sperasse e tentasse, per via di machine far l'istesso effetto senza crescere tardità al mobile, questo certamente rimarrà ingannato"); and the closing shot at "i poco intendenti ingegneri," the little-understanding engineers who dream up such machines "con inganno di tanti principi e con loro propria vergogna," to the deception of so many princes and their own shame. This is the theatre of machines answered from the lecture hall. [P: Favaro edition]
- Galileo on the capstan as a compound machine: an axle with the rope wound on it and a bar in which "the force of a man, or a horse, or another animal born to pull" walks the circumference; the bar is a lever, the barrel an axle, and the rule is the ratio of the two radii, then multiplied by whatever tackle the rope feeds. [P]
- Galileo's three real utilities of machines (same chapter): to move a weight "tutto insieme," all at once, that a small force could only move piece by piece; to work where a person cannot, as pumps do; and to use cheap or inanimate power, "a river, a horse," instead of a man. The compound machine's justification is convenience, never free work. [P]
- Descartes to Huygens, 5 October 1637, "Explication des engins": "the invention of all these engines is founded on only one principle, which is that the same force that can lift a weight of, say, 100 pounds to a height of two feet can also lift one of 200 pounds to a height of one foot, or one of 400 to half a foot," and the engines are "the pulley, the inclined plane, the wedge, the wheel, the screw, the lever, and some others," and one may count more or fewer depending on whether one reduces them to one another. The first modern statement that combining machines changes nothing about the work. [P: AT I, via the Renaissance report]
- Stevin (1605-08): "as the path of the doer is to the path of the sufferer, so is the force of the sufferer to the force of the doer," stated for pulley systems but true of any chain of machines. [P via Dijksterhuis; Mach]
- Wilkins (1648), chapter on the wheel: "by multiplication of which it is easie to move any imaginable weight," and the calculation that a man could move the earth if the wheel ratio matched the ratio of the world's weight to his strength. The Archimedean boast as an exercise in gear ratios. [P]

## Story beats

- Heron's box of wheels: five talents move a thousand, on paper, and Pappus hangs Archimedes' boast on it. [P/S]
- A wall that fishes for ships: beam, chain, iron hand, lead counterweight, a man pressing down the far end, a pulley to let go. [P]
- Sixteen years of an ox walking in a circle, and a gearbox that lets the driver reverse the load without turning the ox around. [W]
- Ramelli's plate 188: a Renaissance reading machine on the gearing of an astronomical clock, for scholars with gout. [S]
- Pigafetta's confession in the margin of the master's book: "we find ourselves deceived, the material making things vary greatly." [P]
- Galileo's parting insult to the engineers who deceive princes. [P]

## Numbers for the illustrator and the physics stage

- Capstan plus tackle: four men at 40 kgf on 2 m bars, barrel 30 cm, feeding a threefold purchase (6): ideal pull 160 x 13.3 x 6, about 12.8 tonnes; at 80 percent for the capstan and 75 percent for the tackle, about 7.7 tonnes; the men walk 80 m round the capstan for each metre the load rises. [derived]
- Heron's 200: four gear pairs of 5 give 625 on paper; his own figures, per Kotsanas, are 5 x 5 x 5 x 8/5 = 200, plus the worm and crank. At 90 percent per mesh the four meshes deliver 66 percent; the worm, perhaps 40 percent; overall a quarter of the ideal. [derived]
- Brunelleschi's hoist: an ox walks about 1 m/s on a whim of, say, 3 m radius; through a 3:1 crown-wheel stage to a drum of 40 cm, the rope rises at about 4 cm/s, a hundred feet in twelve minutes, with the ox pulling about 50 kgf and lifting about 400 kg ideally. Numbers are illustrative; Prager and Scaglia give the measured reconstruction. [derived; U]
- Screw jack: 1.5 mm lead, 300 mm handle, 20 percent efficiency: a 20 kgf push lifts 5 tonnes and the hand travels 1.26 m per 1.5 mm of lift. A jack on a lever handle is already a compound machine. [derived]
- The zip: a wedge (slider) pulled by a lever (the tab) on a chain (the teeth): three machines in a fingertip. [derived]

## Misconceptions to avoid

- "Compound machines multiply work." They multiply force and divide distance, and every stage takes its cut.
- "More stages are always better." Efficiencies multiply down; Heron's train would not turn without the extra reduction, and Guidobaldo warned that the moving power "cannot be given a firm rule."
- "The Renaissance engineers had no theory." They had Vitruvius and Pappus in print and Guidobaldo from 1577; what they lacked was friction, which arrived with Amontons and Coulomb.
- "Archimedes' boast was about a lever." In Pappus it introduces a gear train.
- "The ox hoist was the castello." Two different machines: the hoist on the ground, the crane on the dome.
