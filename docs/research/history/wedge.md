# The wedge

The wedge is the oldest machine and the one theory handled worst. A hand axe is a wedge with no handle; the Greeks explained it as two levers, then as the one power that works by blows; Galileo left it out of his mechanics altogether. It is the inclined plane made to move, and the only one of the six that keeps working after you stop pushing.

## Physics in plain terms

- Ideal mechanical advantage: length of the sloping side divided by the width of the heel. For a symmetric wedge of half-angle t, that is 1 / (2 tan t) per face. Textbooks differ on whether they count one face or both, so quoted formulas differ by a factor of two; say which you mean. [W; S: LibreTexts Engineering Statics 9.1.3]
- It is a moving double inclined plane: the load stays put and the tool moves. Heron drew exactly this contrast in the 1st century: the wedge "acts only by penetrating inward, and so moves the load while the load stays in place; the screw is a twisted wedge that lifts the load to itself while it stays in place." [P: Heron, Mechanics II.17, via Nix]
- A push along the wedge becomes a much larger push across it, perpendicular to each face. Narrow tapers have a larger advantage. [S]
- Friction and self-locking: with a friction angle phi = arctan(mu), a wedge stays in once driven when its half-angle t is less than phi (Wikipedia states it conservatively with the full included angle). That is why a wedge in a log does not spring back between blows, why a doorstop holds, and why a shim stays put. Low-friction wedges split and lift; high-friction wedges hold. [W; S: LibreTexts]
- Why wedges are struck: a steady push cannot exceed the friction plus the material's resistance, but a hammer blow delivers a brief, very large force; the wedge advances a little, self-locking keeps the gain, and the next blow adds to it. It is a ratchet. Heron and Pappus make the same point in different words (below).
- Knife and axe: an axe "is a type of wedge, or dual inclined plane." Cutting axes have shallow angles; splitting axes and mauls are deliberately obtuse so they do not bind in the kerf. A knife's edge only starts the crack; the bevels behind it do the separating. Sharper needs less force because stress is force over contact area and cutting begins where stress exceeds the material's strength. [W: Axe; Cutting]
- Heron's rule of scale: the first three powers must be made larger for larger loads, but "the two remaining, those arising from the wedge and the screw, require that we reduce their size in the same proportion." Smaller angle, less force. [P: Heron II, closing chapter, via Nix]

## Earliest evidence

- Stone tools. Flaked tools at Lomekwi 3, West Turkana, 3.3 million years (Harmand et al., Nature 2015); Oldowan choppers and flakes at Gona, Ethiopia, 2.6 million years (Semaw et al. 1997); Acheulean bifaces at Kokiselei 4, Kenya, 1.76 million years (Lepre et al., Nature 2011). Wikipedia gives 1.6 million for the earliest hand axes; the figures disagree by a hundred thousand years or so. [W; news reports of the papers]
- The hand axe is "perhaps the first example of a wedge" and "the longest-used tool in human history," in use until the middle of the last glacial. [W: Hand axe]
- Hafting. A polished basalt fragment from Carpenter's Gap in the Kimberley, Australia, 44,000-49,000 years old, is the oldest known ground-edge axe (Hiscock, O'Connor, Balme and Maloney, Australian Archaeology 2016); Japan has ground axes from about 35,000-38,000 years (sources differ); most of the world gets hafted axes with farming after 10,000 years ago; Eurasian hafted axes from the Mesolithic (c. 6000 BC). [S: University of Sydney release; W]
- Neolithic polished axes: the Langdale industry in Cumbria, c. 4000-3500 BC, whose polished tuff axes account for about a quarter of examined British Neolithic axes; polishing "improved the mechanical strength of the axe as well as lowering friction when used against wood." [W]
- The plough is a wedge dragged through soil. The Sumerian Farmer's Instructions (known from copies c. 1700 BC) say: "If the barley seed is not being inserted into the hollow of the furrow, change the wedge of your plough share." Ox-drawn ards may go back to the 6th millennium BC. [P: ETCSL 5.6.3; W]

## Quarrying, and the wooden wedge myth

- Aswan. Reginald Engelbach, The Aswan Obelisk (1922), walked the granite quarries and found wedge slots that "always taper inwards" and "appear perfectly smooth inside." On the wet-wood theory: "As to whether these wedges were of wood, and made to expand by wetting, I am not certain, but I believe that they were not, the reason being that the slots always taper inwards, and it appears to me that a wetted wedge would tend to spring out rather than exert a lateral force on the stone." He leaned to hammered metal, probably iron, and cited two iron wedges from the Ramesseum of about 800 BC (Petrie, Tools and Weapons pl. XIII). The unfinished obelisk itself was never touched by a wedge: it was worked by pounding with dolerite balls and by fire, and the wedged-out blocks around it "have been removed at a date later than that of the obelisk." Petrie, by contrast, favoured wetted wooden wedges. [P: archive.org aswnobeliskwiths00egyp]
- The unfinished obelisk: about 41.75 m and about 1090 tons had it been finished, a third larger than any obelisk erected, abandoned when cracks appeared; possibly Hatshepsut's. Tourist figures of 42 m and 1168 tons circulate. [W]
- The wooden wedge, assessed. Mangartz and Pung, "Die Holzkeilspaltung im alten Steinabbau," Der Anschnitt 54 (2002) 238-248, trace the swollen-wood theory to Napoleon's expedition and Aswan, "never really doubted until the 1960s." Josef Roder (1965): the idea "cannot be contradicted energetically enough ... one can split certain schistose stones this way, but not hard rock." Rosemarie Klemm (1984): "gänzlich abwegig" (completely absurd). Dieter Arnold (1994): "purely hypothetical and its effectiveness disputed." Aston, Harrell and Shaw (2000): "Wooden wedges, even when expanded by soaking them with water, would almost certainly not have been strong enough to fracture the granite (although for an extremely laborious but successful attempt see Zuber 1956: 202)." Mangartz and Pung's own view: swelling wood does generate enough pressure and the method is attested ethnographically into the 20th century, but in many easily confused variants (iron-shod wooden wedges, thin boards as feathers for iron wedges, dry plugs watered in drilled holes). Their evidence: a 35 by 40 cm wooden wedge found in an anciently worked part of the Carrara quarries; wedge-pocket rows in Inca quarries, a culture without iron. Present consensus among Egyptologists: wedge splitting in Egypt belongs to the iron-using Late Period and Ptolemaic-Roman times. [S: Deutsches Bergbau-Museum PDF; translations from the German are working translations]
- Greek and Roman. Pliny, Natural History 36.14: Parian marble was quarried by lamplight, and in the Parian quarries "when a single mass of stone was split by the wedges of the quarrymen, the image of a Silenus appeared inside." Pliny 33.72 on gold mining: a hard gravel called gangadia is attacked "with iron wedges and the same hammers," and nothing is thought harder "except the hunger for gold"; flinty rock is broken with fire and vinegar or, because of the smoke, with 150-pound iron crushers (33.71). [P: Latin on LacusCurtius; working translations]
- Roman plug and feathers. In the Roman millstone quarries at Mayen in the Eifel, iron wedges were found "still sitting between two thin wooden boards in the wedge pockets" (Roder, via Mangartz and Pung): plug and feathers 1800 years old. Roman-period wedge pockets with parallel flanks survive on the Drachenfels trachyte. The modern feather-and-tare line of holes was in use on Dartmoor from about 1800; the multi-hole method attributed to a Mr Tarbox of Massachusetts in 1803 "halved quarried stone prices within a year." [S; W: Plug and feather]
- Vitruvius uses wedges only as locks: in stringing catapults the cords are "confined at the holes with wedges, to prevent their slipping" (X.12.2). [P: Morgan]

## Antiquity: the theory

- Pseudo-Aristotle, Mechanical Problems 17 (853a): "Why are great weights and bodies of considerable size split by a small wedge, and why does it exert great pressure? Is it because the wedge consists of two levers opposite to each other? And each has both a weight and a fulcrum, which works either upwards or downwards. The travel of the blow is the weight which strikes and causes movement ... and because it moves an already moving object with considerable speed, the force is even greater." Problem 19 adds that "the axe acts like a wedge; but the wedge, though it is small, splits large pieces of wood, because it is composed of two levers fixed together, and acting in opposite directions." [P: Hett, Loeb]
- Heron, Mechanics II.4 (1st century AD; Arabic of Qusta ibn Luqa; Nix's German, working translation): "The fourth power is the one called the wedge. It is used in some implements of perfume-making, and to join separate pieces of carpentry. Its uses are manifold, but most often we use it when we intend to split the underside of stones we want to quarry, after we have already freed the sides from the mountain. Here none of the other powers works, not even if they were all combined; the wedge alone works here. Its action rests on the blow that strikes it, whatever the nature of the blow, and its action does not cease when the blow ceases. This is clear, for often a noise arises from it without its being struck, and a bursting of what it splits with its force. The more acute the angle of the wedge, the easier it is to work with, as we shall show." [P via Nix 1900]
- Heron II.14-15: the blow "acts only through the contact, which does not remain on the wedge for even the least time. From this it is seen that the wedge moves after the blow has ceased ... some time after the blow, noises and crackings come from the wedge," like a thrown stone that flies on after leaving the hand; and "every blow, even a light one, moves every wedge ... The smaller the angle of the wedge becomes, the smaller can the force be." [P via Nix]
- Heron II.5 and 17: the screw "is only a twisted wedge, which however cannot be struck, but is moved by means of the lever"; "turning takes the place of striking." [P]
- Pappus, Collection VIII (c. AD 320), repeats Heron almost verbatim: the wedge is of great use "for perfume presses and for the excellent gluing of timbers which carpenters use, and, what is greatest of all, when in quarries the lower parts of stones must be torn from the continuous mass"; "even if the wedge is not struck, by its own force sounds and ruptures sometimes occur"; the screw is "a twisted wedge, free of percussion" (cuneus tortus, percussionis expers, in Commandino's Latin). Hultsch's note explains that several men strike the wedge "by turns" while "the wedge exercises its own power without interruption." [P: Hultsch 1878, Latin; working translation]
- What is missing from all of them is a formula. The Greeks knew that acute is easier and that blows matter; the ratio of length to width as a mechanical advantage is a post-Galilean textbook statement, once the plane had its law.

## Medieval

- Wood: the beetle (Old English bietel, a hammer) is the mallet that drives wedges; the froe splits or rives boards along the grain and is struck with a froe club. Nordic clinker boats used cleft, radially split oak planks because riven timber is stronger than sawn, at the cost of getting planks no wider than about a third of the log's diameter. [W: Froe; Clinker (boat building)]
- Villard de Honnecourt (c. 1225-1235) draws a water-powered saw, not a wedge machine; Jordanus, who solved the inclined plane, never treats the wedge; the Banu Musa write about fountains and valves. The medieval wedge is a craft, not a theory. [W]
- Mining practice, as Agricola later recorded it (1556, describing long-established Saxon methods), Book V: hard ore "they break with fires" if the neighbours allow; otherwise "they drive into one of the little cracks one of the iron tools ... then in each fracture they place four thin iron blocks, and in order to hold them more firmly ... as many thin iron plates back to back; next they place thinner iron plates between each two iron blocks, and strike and drive them by turns with hammers, whereby the vein rings with a shrill sound; and the moment when it begins to be detached ... a tearing sound is heard. As soon as this grows distinct the miners hastily flee away; then a great crash is heard as the vein is broken and torn, and falls down. By this method they throw down a portion of a vein weighing a hundred pounds more or less." Hoover's note: fire-setting "persisted in certain German and Norwegian mines down to the end of the 19th century." [P: Hoover translation, Gutenberg 38015]

## Renaissance

- Agricola, Book VI, with the woodcut (Hoover's illustration 150: "E - Wedge. F - Iron block. G - Iron plate"): "A wedge is usually three palms and two digits long and six digits wide; at the upper end, for a distance of a palm, it is three digits thick, and beyond that point it becomes thinner by degrees, until finally it is quite sharp." The largest two-handed hammers, "five digits wide and thick, and a foot long," are the ones with which "they drive the wedges into the cracks"; handles are kept light "that the workmen may be able to strike more powerful blows." Timbering: "wooden wedges or poles cut from trees are driven in between the timbers and the vein." [P]
- Leonardo, Codex Madrid I (1490s). Folio 44v, a "hammer lift" that raises a weight by "consecutive strikes by three wedge-shaped hammers," where he "studied the length of the constituent parts to ensure that the machine worked continuously and at a steady pace"; folio 111v, a wooden wedge "shaped into a very sharp dihedral angle, to fit one object to another, making it impossible to separate them," recommended for steadying furniture legs. Any sentence of Leonardo's calling the wedge an inclined plane, or a wedge-driven olive press, is unverified. [S: BNE and Google Arts exhibition text; U]
- Guidobaldo del Monte, Mechanicorum liber (Pesaro 1577), De cuneo. The one chapter with no numbered propositions. He takes from the Mechanical Problems that "the two flanks of a wedge can split other material because they act as two levers," and adds that the flanks "can also be considered as inclined planes which in turn can again be reduced to the lever." But he sees the difficulty: as the wedge splits, "the fulcrums of these levers themselves move and thus fail to maintain a constant ratio," and the inclined plane theory he had was Pappus's wrong one, so "with a wedge, according to Guidobaldo, any given power cannot move any given weight." He notes that blows matter but that "no theory available to Guidobaldo ... allowed for a calculation of the relation between force and effect" of percussion. Heron's programme breaks on the wedge. [S: Renn and Damerow 2010, section 3.8; Laird 2013]
- Galileo, Le Mecaniche (c. 1600), has no wedge chapter. The word cuneo does not occur in the text. The screw is derived from the inclined plane, and the closing chapter on the force of percussion opens with a wedge in all but name: "Percotendosi sopra un chiodo da ficcarsi in un durissimo legno, o vero sopra un palo che debbia penetrare dentro in terreno ben fisso, si vede, per la sola virtù della percossa, spingersi e l'uno e l'altro avanti" (striking a nail to be driven into very hard wood, or a stake that must go into firm ground, one sees both driven forward by the force of the blow alone). He promised a theory of percussion and never finished it. [P: Favaro edition, it.wikisource]
- Cardano, De subtilitate (1550), Book XVII on the arts covers mechanics; a wedge passage is not verified. Besson (1578): plate 10 is a stone saw, plate 59 a press; no wedge machine. Ramelli and Zonca: no wedge device verified. The Pompeian fresco of cupids working a wedge press (House of the Vettii) is often cited and not confirmed this session. [U]

## The key insight and who had it

- Pseudo-Aristotle (c. 300 BC): the wedge is two levers back to back, and the blow adds the speed of the hammer to the weight of the wedge.
- Heron (1st century AD): the wedge is the power that works by blows and goes on working between them; acute is easier; the screw is a wedge wound up.
- Guidobaldo (1577): the honest failure. The lever model breaks because the fulcrum moves; the plane model was not yet right; percussion had no theory.
- Galileo (c. 1600): the plane gets its law and the wedge becomes its moving form, but he leaves the chapter unwritten and turns to percussion.
- The formula on the site, length over width, is the 18th-century textbook settlement once the plane's law was accepted.

## Story beats

- Heron's creaking wedge: a quarry wedge that groans and cracks in the stone after the hammers stop, the one machine that works while you rest. [P]
- Pliny's Silenus: a Parian block split by wedges opens to show a figure of Silenus inside. [P]
- Agricola's miners: iron wedges and plates struck by turns "until the vein rings with a shrill sound," then a tearing noise, and the miners run before "a great crash." [P]
- Engelbach at Aswan in 1922: wedge slots that taper inwards, polished smooth, and a thousand-ton obelisk that no wedge ever touched. [P]
- Mayen: Roman iron wedges found still seated between two thin boards in their slots, plug and feathers eighteen centuries old. [S]

## Descendants

- Knife, axe, chisel, adze, plane iron, needle, pin, nail (the Roman hoard at Inchtuthil, seven tons of nails abandoned in AD 86-87), the ploughshare, the ship's bow, the splitting maul and the hydraulic log splitter (a ram drives a wedge). [W]
- The zip: Judson's clasp locker (1893), Sundback's hookless fastener (sold 1913, patented 1917), named "zipper" by Goodrich in 1923. The slider is a Y-shaped wedge that meshes or parts the teeth as it moves. [W]
- Holding wedges: doorstops, shims, wheel chocks, tent pegs, the wedge anchor (tightening a bolt drives a wedge up into a sleeve that expands against the hole). [W]
- Snowploughs: "the first snowplows were horse-drawn wedge-plows made of wood," recorded in New Hampshire in 1792; first motor plough 1913. [W]
- Surgery: the closing-wedge osteotomy removes a wedge of bone to realign a knee; dental wedges hold matrix bands for fillings. [W; U for dentistry]

## Misconceptions to avoid

- "A knife cuts by sharpness alone." The edge starts the crack; the wedge behind it separates. A thin edge on a thick bevel binds.
- "Thinner is always better." Heron says acute is easier, but a too-thin wedge binds and breaks; splitting mauls are obtuse on purpose; Engelbach's slots taper inward; feathers keep the wedge tip off the bottom of the slot.
- "The Egyptians split granite with wet wooden wedges." Contested since the 1960s and rejected by most Egyptologists for hard rock; the Aswan obelisk was pounded and burned, not wedged.
- "Wedge and inclined plane are the same thing." Same geometry, opposite roles: on a ramp the load moves, with a wedge the tool moves. Heron already drew the line.
- "The wedge is two levers." Pseudo-Aristotle's model; Guidobaldo showed why it fails (the fulcrum moves).
- "Galileo covered all six." He omitted the wedge.

## Numbers for the illustrator and the physics stage

- A splitting wedge 20 cm long and 4 cm wide at the heel: advantage 5 per face on the ideal count; a 30 kg sledge blow becomes about 150 kgf across each face. With friction 0.4 and a half-angle of 5.7 degrees the wedge is self-locking (5.7 is less than the friction angle of 21.8), so it stays in between blows. [derived]
- A kitchen knife with a 15-degree bevel per side: advantage about 1.9 per face at the bevel, but the contact area at the edge is a few square micrometres wide by the cut length, which is why the stress at the edge is enormous and the force needed small. [derived]
- Agricola's wedge, three palms two digits long by six digits wide by three digits thick at the head, is roughly 26 by 11 by 5.5 cm, which puts the included angle near 12 degrees and the per-face advantage near 5. [derived from Hoover's text]
- Heron's scale rule: double the load, and for the wedge you do not double the tool, you halve its angle.
