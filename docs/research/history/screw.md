# The screw

The screw is the inclined plane wound round a post. It is the machine with the largest advantage for its size and the worst efficiency, and the two facts are one fact: the friction that wastes most of the effort is what makes a screw hold. It is also the machine whose story splits in two, the water screw that lifts by turning and the thread that presses, clamps and fastens, and both are tied to Archimedes by writers who lived long after him.

Reliability tags: [P] primary source seen in text or translation, [S] scholarly secondary, [W] Wikipedia-level or museum-label level, [U] unverified or recalled, check before use.

## Physics in plain terms

- A screw is a helix, an inclined plane wrapped on a cylinder. Turn it once and it advances by its lead. Ideal mechanical advantage: MA = 2 pi r / lead, where r is the radius at which the effort is applied, the handle or wrench arm. Long handle, fine thread, large advantage. [W; textbook]
- Pitch is the distance between adjacent threads; lead is the advance per turn. Single-start: lead = pitch. Multi-start: lead = pitch times the number of starts. Confusing them is the commonest error in screw arithmetic. [W: Screw (simple machine); Leadscrew]
- Lead angle: tan(lambda) = lead / (pi d), with d the mean thread diameter. The helix angle is its complement, another source of confusion. [textbook]
- Efficiency: eta = tan(lambda) / tan(lambda + phi), where tan(phi) = mu. Well-lubricated jack screws manage only 15 to 20 percent; lead screws 25 to 70; ball screws about 90, which is why ball screws back-drive and jacks do not. [W]
- Self-locking: a screw holds its load without a brake if and only if the lead angle is less than the friction angle, equivalently if and only if its efficiency is below 50 percent. A clamp, a jack and a bolt live on the wrong side of that line on purpose. Multi-start lead screws and ball screws live on the right side and need brakes; vibration defeats nominal self-locking, which is why lock washers exist. [W]
- Thread forms: 60-degree V (ISO metric, Unified, Sellers); Whitworth (55 degrees, rounded crest and root); square (most efficient, hard to cut); Acme (29 degrees included, proposed by Albert Man Powell in American Machinist, 24 January 1895, easier to cut than square and adjustable for wear; Wikipedia dates it 1894); buttress (one flank nearly square for one-way loads). [W]
- The water screw is a different machine with the same shape. It does not trade force for distance through a nut; it is a rotating helical bucket chain in which every pocket of water is carried upward while, locally, it always runs downhill. Galileo saw this and called it miraculous (below). [P]
- Heron's scale rule: for wedge and screw, a bigger load calls for a smaller angle, that is, closer turns, not a bigger tool. "The screw whose turns are closer moves the load more easily." [P: Mechanics II.17 via Nix]

## Earliest evidence

- The water screw. Diodorus Siculus (1st century BC), I.34: the Delta's people irrigate "by means of a contrivance which was invented by Archimedes of Syracuse and is called, after its shape, a screw"; V.37: the Spanish silver mines are drained "by means of what is called by men the Egyptian screw, which was invented by Archimedes of Syracuse at the time of his visit to Egypt ... an enormous amount of water is thrown out, to one's astonishment, by means of a trifling amount of labour." [P: Oldfather, Loeb]
- Athenaeus, Deipnosophistae V.208f, quoting Moschion on Hiero's Syracusia (c. 240 BC): "the hold, although of a most enormous depth, was pumped out by one man, by means of the screw, an engine which was the contrivance of Archimedes." Moschion is lost and his date uncertain. [P: Yonge]
- Strabo, Geography XVII.1.30: at Babylon near Memphis water "is conducted up from the river by wheels and screws; and one hundred and fifty prisoners are employed in the work"; XVII.1.52: the high parts of the Nile islands "are irrigated by means of screws." No inventor named. [P]
- Vitruvius X.6 (c. 30-20 BC), the only ancient construction manual: "a beam is selected, the thickness of which in digits is equivalent to its length in feet" (sixteen digits to the foot, so the core is sixteen times longer than thick); its ends are divided by compass "into eight parts, by quadrants and octants"; pitch-coated willow withes laid obliquely across the grid form "spiral channels which naturally look just like those of a snail shell"; layers are built up until the diameter is an eighth of the length; the whole is boarded over, shod with iron at the ends, set at the slope of "the Pythagorean right-angled triangle: that is, let its length be divided into five parts; let three of them denote the height," and "turned by the treading of men." Vitruvius does not name Archimedes. [P: Morgan; Latin on LacusCurtius]
- Did it predate Archimedes? Stephanie Dalley reads a Sennacherib inscription (early 7th century BC) as describing bronze-cast water screws for his Nineveh garden, "some centuries before the time of Archimedes" (Dalley and Oleson, Technology and Culture 44, 2003; Dalley 2013). The argument is philological, a re-read passage, not archaeological, and Wikipedia calls it "contentious among academics." Oleson's broader caution: there is "the total lack of any literary or archaeological evidence for the existence of the water-screw before ca. 250 BC." No ancient source links the screw to Sesostris. Report as contested. [S: OUP blog by Dalley; W]
- The thread. Archytas of Tarentum (c. 400 BC) is often credited with inventing the screw; Diogenes Laertius VIII.83 says only that he "was the first to bring mechanics to a system by applying mathematical principles" and used "a section of a half-cylinder" in a geometric construction. The word screw does not occur. The attribution is a 19th-century encyclopedia habit repeated without a source. [P: Diogenes Laertius; W]
- Screw presses. Pliny, Natural History XVIII.317 (Rackham): "In old days, people used to drag down the pressbeams with ropes and leather straps, and by means of levers. But within the last hundred years, the Greek pattern of press has been invented, with the grooves of the upright beam running spirally," some with a star and others with the tree raising boxes of stones; and "within the last 22 years" a smaller press with a short central screw pressing directly down on drums. Burton and Lewit (Klio 2019) argue the passage has been mistranslated for eighty years and that Pliny gives dates of first use, not a replacement sequence; "Graecanica" means Greek-style screws, alluding to Archimedes' helix. Pompeii's House of the Vettii fresco shows cupids working a screw press with a detachable handle; a screw-operated garment press was excavated at Herculaneum (Maiuri 1932). [P via Gichon; S: Burton and Lewit abstract]
- Surviving Roman screws (Sim and Legg, EXARC): square threads on Roman jewellery, a bronze lamp stand from Herculaneum with V-thread fastenings (before AD 79), a 157 mm iron wood screw from Silchester, square threads on vaginal specula. Their experiment: hand-filing a V-thread took about an hour and three quarters. [S]

## Antiquity: the theory

- Heron, Mechanics II.1 and II.5 (1st century AD; Arabic of Qusta ibn Luqa; Nix's German, working translations): the fifth power is the screw, "only a twisted wedge, which however cannot be struck, but is moved by means of the lever." He defines the helix kinematically, a point running along the side of a cylinder while the side makes one revolution, and gives the wrapping construction: take two perpendicular lines, one equal to the side of the cylinder and one to its circumference, join their ends, lay the figure on the cylinder, and "the side opposite the right angle wraps onto the surface of the cylinder and produces one turn of thread"; divide the side into equal parts for many turns. [P via Nix]
- Heron II.17: "we must think of the screw only as a twisted wedge, because the triangle that determines the thread has the shape of the wedge ... turning takes the place of striking ... the wedge acts only by penetrating inward and so moves the load while the load stays in place; the screw is a twisted wedge that lifts the load to itself while remaining in place." [P via Nix]
- Heron III.13-21: single-screw and twin-screw presses; the beam of the twin-screw press has "two round openings in which there is a thread, so that they form the female screws, so that when both screws are turned the wood sinks"; the last chapter is a screw-cutter for cutting a female thread in a plank, "necessary for direct screw presses." Drachmann reconstructed it (JHS 56, 1936); Kenny called the reconstruction "technically impossible"; Drachmann replied that only experiment could settle it. [P via Nix; S: Drachmann; DSB]
- The endless screw. Heron's baroulkos (Mechanics I.1; Dioptra 37): after a train of toothed wheels, "place a screw next to wheel XW, the thread fitting the teeth of the wheel ... fit it with a crank ... the force operating at the crank is even more effective, for the crank describes a circle greater than the perimeter of the screw." The Dioptra uses a small screw meshing with a toothed wheel for fine adjustment. This is the worm gear, and it appears in Pappus VIII proposition 10 as the way to move a given weight with a given force. [P: Cohen and Drabkin pp. 227-228, 336; S: Heath II]
- Pappus VIII (c. AD 320): "the screw is merely a spiral-shaped wedge, which does not receive blows but is set in motion by a lever" (Cohen and Drabkin); Commandino's Latin, "cuneus tortus, percussionis expers." The book ends with how to cut a screw thread and a toothed wheel to mesh with it. [P; S]

## Medieval

- The water screw drops out of the Western record after the 3rd century and reappears in Konrad Kyeser's Bellifortis (1405). [W]
- Al-Jazari's Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices (1206) has five water-raising machines, chain pumps, bucket machines and a crank-driven double-acting piston pump, but no screw. The "crank-driven Archimedes screw of al-Jazari" is probably a confusion; treat as [U]. [W]
- Gutenberg (c. 1440) "adopted the basic design of the screw press" of the Roman wine and olive press: the printer "pulled a long lever, the bar, which drove a screw that pressed the platen onto the paper." By 1600 a press made about 240 impressions an hour. [W]
- Metal screw fasteners were "rare in Europe before the 15th century, if known at all." The first documented screwdriver is in the Housebook of Wolfegg Castle (1475-1490); the tool is a Schraubendreher in German and a tournevis, a turnscrew, in French, and its first uses were plate armour and matchlock and wheellock guns. Taps and dies are drawn in the Löffelholz codex (Nuremberg 1505); Joseph Clement sold them commercially from 1828. Rybczynski's One Good Turn (2000) traces the tool through armour and firearms. [W; S via reviews]
- Villard de Honnecourt's screw jack, Taccola's screws, and the medieval bone-setting screw are not verified. Francesco di Giorgio's Trattato (after 1482) contains screw jacks and column-raising machines, but the specific plates were not checked. [U]

## Renaissance

- Leonardo. A thread-cutting machine with two lead screws geared to a crank that also turns the blank, and change wheels for different pitches; the Science Museum's model (1952-281) sources it to MS B f. 70v (1488-89) rather than the Codex Atlanticus f. 1103r that is often cited, and notes that Leonardo drew all three screws left-handed, "suggesting the machine was an original conception quickly noted down with an error in detail." Codex Madrid I (1490s) is "the first monographic treatise on mechanical elements"; the Museo Leonardiano models a worm-screw jack from f. 26r. His tap-and-die drawings are not verified. [W: Science Museum Group; Museo Leonardiano; U]
- Besson, Theatrum instrumentorum (1578): plates 7-8 are swash-plate lathes, plate 9 is the screw-cutting lathe, plate 59 a press. [S: Smithsonian Dibner Library]
- Ramelli (1588): the Smithsonian tally counts 14 "screw jacks and other breaking devices" among the 195 plates. Plates 155-156: a screw jack for lifting a door off its hinges, "operated by a single person, easily and with little noise," with worm and worm gear; plates 162-167: screw jacks for breaking door bolts and bending gratings; plate 181: a weight-moving machine with worm and worm gear; plate 188: the bookwheel with epicyclic gearing. The "170-175" sometimes cited for the jacks is wrong. [S: Science History Institute; Smithsonian]
- Guidobaldo del Monte, Mechanicorum liber (Pesaro 1577), De cochlea (ff. 120-128; Italian by Pigafetta 1581; English in Drake and Drabkin, Mechanics in Sixteenth-Century Italy, 1969, pp. 325-326). He opens by noting that Pappus promised, but never delivered, a proof that the screw is a wedge moved by a lever; shows that a wedge ABC "turned around a cylinder" gives a two-helix screw; then prefers the inclined plane: "Si fuerit cochlea AB helices habens aequales CDEFG. Dico has nihil aliud esse praeter planum horizonti inclinatum circa cylindrum revolutum" (if the screw AB has equal helices CDEFG, I say these are nothing but a plane inclined to the horizon wound round a cylinder), and refers the plane to Pappus's reduction to the balance, which is the flawed step. Corollary: more threads per length and a longer handle make the load easier, and slower, to move. His posthumous De cochlea (1615) is a separate work. [S: Palmieri 2008; Renn and Damerow 2010; W]
- Galileo, Le Mecaniche, "Della vite" (c. 1600). Opening: "la vite tenere il primo luogo; come quella che non solo al muovere, ma al fermare e stringere con forza grandissima, acconciamente si adatta" (the screw holds first place, as the one that suits not only moving but holding and clamping with very great force). Then the inclined plane law, then: "la forma ed essenza primaria della vite non è altro che un simil triangolo ACB, il quale spinto inanzi, sottentra al grave da alzarsi" (the primary form and essence of the screw is nothing but such a triangle ACB which, pushed forward, gets under the weight to be raised); the inventor "preso il medesimo triangolo, lo circondò ed avvolse intorno al cilindro ABCD ... ed il piano ascendente generava sopra il detto cilindro la linea elica" (took the same triangle and wound it round the cylinder, the rising plane generating the helix on it); the nut: "se li accomoda la sua madre-vite con la elice incavata; nella quale entrando il maschio, cioè il verme della vite, voltata poi intorno, solleva ed inalza la madre" (fit it with its mother-screw with the hollowed helix; the male, the worm of the screw, enters it and, turned, lifts and raises the mother); and the law: "la forza nella vite ABCD multiplicarsi secondo la proporzione che la lunghezza di tutto il verme AEFGH eccede l'altezza CB" (the force in the screw is multiplied in the proportion by which the whole length of the thread exceeds the height). [P: Favaro edition]
- Galileo, "Della coclea d'Archimede per levar l'acqua": the machine "non solo è maravigliosa, ma è miracolosa" (is not only marvellous but miraculous), because "l'acqua ascende nella vite discendendo continuamente" (the water rises in the screw while continually descending). Wrap the sloping channel on a cylinder and tilt the cylinder by more than the channel's slope ("la salita del canale AC verrà tolta via abbassandolo dalla parte C per la terza parte di un angolo retto," the channel's climb is cancelled by lowering it at C by a third of a right angle), and every stretch of channel runs downhill while the whole carries water up: "la vite per alzar l'acqua deve esser inclinata un poco più della quantità dell'angolo del triangolo" (the screw must be tilted a little more than the triangle's angle). He closes: "il che di quanta meraviglia si sia, lascio giudicare a chi perfettamente l'averà inteso" (how marvellous this is, I leave to the judgement of whoever has perfectly understood it). [P]
- Agricola (1556) shows no screws among the mining machines; Zonca (1607) has an endless screw and winch plate, and presses. [W; U for Zonca's presses]

## Later

- The Wyatt brothers patented a screw-making machine in Staffordshire in 1760; by the 1780s their factory made "16,000 screws a day with only 30 employees." Ramsden's screw-cutting lathe, 1777. [W]
- Henry Maudslay perfected his screw-cutting lathe about 1797-1800: lead screw, slide rest and change gears, the triad that made threads reproducible. The Science Museum's lathe (object 1900-20) has "a screw feed and a micrometer dial" on the slide rest and a leadscrew "geared to the lathe mandrel by change wheels (now missing)"; before it "screw threads were crudely manufactured by hand." His bench micrometer, "the Lord Chancellor" (c. 1805), settled disputes in the shop. His pupils included Whitworth, Nasmyth and Clement. [W: Science Museum Group]
- Joseph Whitworth, 1841: a 55-degree thread with rounded crest and root and a standard pitch for each diameter, "the first nationally standardised system," spread by the railway companies. [W]
- William Sellers, 1864: 60 degrees with flat crest and root, "easier for ordinary mechanics and machinists to cut," presented at the Franklin Institute (April per ASME, September per Wikipedia) and endorsed in December; the basis of the later Unified thread. [S: ASME landmark; W]
- Palmer's handheld micrometer 1848; Brown and Sharpe 1867. Ball screw: US patents 601,451 and 610,044 (1898); the "Boehm 1929" claim conflicts. Phillips cross-head popularised in the early 1930s. [W; U for ball screw priority]
- "The screw is the most produced machine element in the world" is plausible and unsupported by any reliable statistic found. [U]

## The key insight and who had it

- Heron (1st century AD): the screw is a wedge wound round a cylinder, moved by turning instead of striking; closer turns, easier lift; the worm gear as the way to move any load with any force.
- Guidobaldo (1577): the helix is an inclined plane wound on a cylinder; more turns and a longer handle, easier and slower.
- Galileo (c. 1600): the same, but with the plane's correct law, so the screw's advantage is thread length over rise; and the water screw explained as a channel that always runs downhill.
- The self-locking condition and the efficiency formula are 19th-century machine-design results, once Coulomb's friction was in hand.

## Story beats

- One man empties the bilge of the largest ship in the world with a screw, and the same engineer launches the hull with a helix, in a story told four centuries after the fact. [P: Athenaeus]
- Galileo's paradox: water climbs a screw by always flowing downhill, and he leaves the reader to marvel. [P]
- Gutenberg turns a wine press into a book machine by adding a bar. [W]
- Leonardo draws a change-gear thread-cutting lathe three centuries early, with every thread left-handed. [W: Science Museum]
- Maudslay's lathe survives with its micrometer dial and without its change wheels, and the shop it ran bred the men who standardised the thread. [W: Science Museum]

## Descendants

- Bolts, nuts and wood screws (Silchester onward); jar lids and bottle caps; corkscrews; vices and clamps (Galileo's "stringere"); screw jacks (Ramelli) and car jacks; presses for oil, wine, print and coin; the micrometer and the telescope's fine screws (Gascoigne, 17th century; Palmer 1848); lead screws and ball screws in CNC machines; augers and drills; propellers; spiral staircases; conveyor augers, grain elevators, snowblowers, combine harvesters, extruders and injection moulders. [W]
- The water screw today: sewage headworks pumps, because they "cope well with varying rates of flow and with suspended solids" and run for decades; Dutch polder drainage (Kinderdijk); soil extraction in the 2001 stabilisation of the Leaning Tower of Pisa; and, run backwards, hydro turbines (Radlik patent 1991; Brada tests 1993-95) for heads of 1 to 8 m at 5 to 500 kW, with efficiencies of 60 to 80 percent and slow rotation that lets fish pass, as at Settle, Torrs and Windsor Castle. [W; industry pages]

## Misconceptions to avoid

- "Screws are always self-locking." Only if the lead angle is below the friction angle; ball screws and multi-start screws back-drive.
- "The Archimedes screw and the screw fastener are the same machine." Same helix, different job; the water screw carries, the thread presses.
- "Pitch equals lead." Only for single-start threads.
- "The screw is the strongest simple machine." Largest advantage per size, lowest efficiency; the loss is the point.
- "Archimedes invented the screw." Diodorus and Athenaeus say so, two centuries later; Vitruvius, Philo and Strabo describe it without him; Dalley's Assyrian claim is contested.
- "Archytas invented the screw thread." No ancient text says so.
- "Galileo's screw chapter is about fasteners." It is about the jack and the press: moving, holding and clamping.

## Numbers for the illustrator and the physics stage

- A jack with a 1.5 mm lead and a 300 mm handle: ideal advantage 2 pi 300 / 1.5, about 1257. At 20 percent efficiency, about 250: a 20 kgf push lifts 5 tons, and the handle travels 1257 mm of arc for each 1.5 mm of lift. [derived]
- An M10 bolt (lead 1.5 mm, mean diameter about 9 mm) has a lead angle of about 3 degrees; with mu 0.15 the friction angle is 8.5 degrees, so it holds; efficiency near 25 percent. [derived]
- Vitruvius's screw: a core 1 foot thick and 16 feet long, with eight channels, set at 3-4-5, so the axis rises 3 units per 5 of length, about 37 degrees. [P; derived]
- Galileo's rule: force multiplied by thread length over height. A thread 1 m long climbing 10 cm gives 10. [P]
