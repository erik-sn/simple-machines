# The pulley

A rope over a wheel changes the direction of a pull; a rope through several wheels divides a load among its strands. The second idea is the compound pulley, the polyspaston, and it is the machine with which Archimedes, in the most famous demonstration in the history of mechanics, hauled a loaded ship across a beach by hand. The story is usually told for the lever; it belongs here.

Reliability tags: [P] primary source seen in text or translation, [S] scholarly secondary, [W] Wikipedia-level or museum-label level, [U] unverified or recalled, check before use.

## Physics in plain terms

- Fixed pulley: mechanical advantage 1. It changes the direction of the pull, so a person can pull down with their weight instead of lifting up. Galileo, Le Mecaniche, "Delle taglie": the upper sheave "non apportare facilità alcuna alla forza semplicemente considerata, ma solamente al modo di applicarla" (brings no ease to the force as such, only to the way of applying it). [P]
- Movable pulley: the load hangs from two strands of rope, so each carries half. Galileo: "la forza in F esser la metà del peso da lei sostenuto." [P]
- Block and tackle: ideal advantage equals the number of rope strands that support the moving block. Count strands at the moving block, not sheaves. The hauling strand counts only if it leaves the moving block ("rove to advantage"); if it leaves the fixed block ("rove to disadvantage") it does not. [W: Block and tackle; textbook]
- Galileo counts strands too: the weight "vien fermato dalle tre corde ... le quali con eguale fatica sostengono il peso" (is held by the three ropes, which sustain the weight with equal effort). Stevin, in the pulley section of his 1605 supplement, has "het ghewicht B dan hangende an drie tauwen, die elck een derdendeel draghen" (the weight B hanging on three ropes, each carrying a third), and continues to four. [P]
- Sailors' names: gun tackle, 2 strands (a single block at each end); luff tackle, 3 (double and single); double tackle, 4; gyn tackle, 5; threefold purchase, 6. Rove to advantage adds one. [W]
- The trade: to raise the load by one metre every supporting strand shortens by a metre, so the hauler pulls in n metres of rope. Galileo: "quanto si guadagna di forza, tanto perdersi di velocità" (as much as is gained in force, that much is lost in speed), and "il viaggio che fa la forza venga ad essere doppio del movimento del peso" for the single movable pulley. [P]
- Stevin's general rule, from the pulley treatise in the Wisconstighe Gedachtenissen (1605-08): "Ghelijck wech des doenders tot wech des lijders, alsoo ghewelt des lijders tot ghewelt des doenders" (as the path of the doer is to the path of the sufferer, so is the force of the sufferer to the force of the doer). In the Latin Hypomnemata: "Ut spatium agentis ad spatium patientis, sic potentia patientis ad potentiam agentis." Mach called this "the germ of the principle of virtual displacements." Note it is in the 1605-08 supplement, not the 1586 Weeghdaet. [P: DBNL text; Mach; S: Dijksterhuis]
- Friction: every sheave loses a few percent to axle friction and rope stiffness; there is a point where another sheave costs more than it gains. Coulton judged six sheaves, advantage 6, "the useful maximum if natural fibre ropes are used." [S: Coulton 1974, p. 12; W]
- A sheave is a lever. Galileo: "la girella circondotta doventa una libra simile alla AC, ma perpetuata" (the sheave turned about becomes a balance like AC, but made perpetual). Guidobaldo built his whole pulley chapter on this reduction. [P; S]
- Differential (Weston) pulley: two sheaves of radii R and r fixed on one axle and an endless chain; advantage 2R/(R - r); self-locking under load. Patented in Britain in 1859 by Thomas Aldridge Weston, exhibited 1861-62, made by Tangye of Birmingham, and still the basis of the hand chain hoist. Wikipedia says invented 1854; the patent is 1859. [W: Differential pulley; Grace's Guide]

## Earliest evidence

- Assyria. Coulton: "The simple pulley, used not to gain mechanical advantage but just to change the direction of pull, is first known from an Assyrian relief of the ninth century B.C.," hauling a bucket of water, and "there is no evidence that the system was further developed." A relief from the palace of Tiglath-pileser III at Nimrud (8th century BC, British Museum) shows a pulley with two ropes inside a besieged city. Whether these are one tradition or two is unclear; say "by the 9th-8th century BC." [S: Coulton 1974; W]
- Egypt. Wikipedia claims pulleys in the 12th Dynasty (1991-1802 BC), citing Arnold's Building in Egypt; Coulton says "it is virtually certain that Egyptian architects of the third and second millennia B.C. did not use any kind of pulley system." Arnold's objects are probably grooved wooden rope rollers without a rotating sheave. Treat the Egyptian pulley as contested. [W; S; U for Arnold's wording]
- Sennacherib's reliefs (c. 700 BC) are not pulley evidence: the winged bulls move "with levers, a sledge and rollers, and an artificial ramp ... there is no sign of a block and tackle or a winch." [S: Coulton, p. 10]
- Greek temples. Cuttings for lifting tongs and lewis irons first appear in buildings of the late 6th century BC (Treasury of the Athenians at Delphi, Treasury of the Megarians at Olympia, the old Propylaia at Athens, the Alkmaionid temple at Delphi, Aphaia at Aigina); "none need be earlier than c. 515 B.C.," and "after c. 515 B.C. ... cranes were in common use." Then a striking consequence: at least thirteen blocks over 20 tons were set in the 6th century, and "in the fifth century no blocks weighing over twenty tons were used." The crane made builders cut smaller stones. [S: Coulton, "Lifting in early Greek architecture," JHS 94 (1974) 1-19]
- Pulleys, plural, appear in an Athenian building inscription of 329/8 BC (IG II2 1672.156). [S: Coulton n. 6]
- The word: trochilia or trochilea, a small wheel; Heron's list has trochilos; the compound pulley is polyspaston, "much-drawing." Vitruvius Latinises trispastos, pentaspastos, polyspastos. [P]
- The theatre crane (mechane) of the 5th century BC lowered gods onto the stage; the earliest secure use is Euripides' Medea (431 BC). Aristophanes, Peace 174-175 (421 BC): Trygaeus on his dung beetle begs the "machinist" to take care of him. Whether it used sheaves or a beam and counterweight is not known. [W; P for Aristophanes]

## Antiquity

- Pseudo-Aristotle, Mechanical Problems 18 (853a32-b13) is "the earliest indisputable evidence for a knowledge of compound pulley systems" (Coulton). Several pulleys progressively reduce the weight to be moved. [P; S]
- Plutarch, Marcellus 14.7-8 (Perrin). Archimedes, "a kinsman and friend of King Hiero," wrote that "with any given force it was possible to move any given weight" and boasted that with another world to stand on he could move this one. Hiero "begged him to put his proposition into execution, and show him some great weight moved by a slight force." Archimedes took "a three-masted merchantman of the royal fleet, which had been dragged ashore by the great labours of many men, and after putting on board many passengers and the customary freight," he "seated himself at a distance from her, and without any great effort, but quietly setting in motion with his hand a system of compound pulleys, drew her towards him smoothly and evenly, as though she were gliding through the water." The Greek word is polyspastou. Hiero, "comprehending the power of his art," ordered war engines. [P]
- Two readings to avoid: "lifted with a single pulley" comes from Dryden's loose English, "only holding the head of the pulley in his hand"; and the ship was hauled along the shore, not lifted. [P: Dryden; Perrin]
- Athenaeus, Deipnosophistae V.206d-209e, quoting Moschion, tells a different story about a different ship: Archimedes launched Hiero's giant Syracusia "by himself with the aid of a few persons," having "prepared a windlass [helix]," and its bilge was pumped by one man "by means of the screw, an engine which was the contrivance of Archimedes." The Greek helix is ambiguous between a screw and a winding device. Keep the two stories apart. [P: Yonge; U for the scholarly discussion of helix]
- Heron, Mechanics II.1-3 (1st century AD): the pulley is the third power, "also called the 'multi-lifter' [polyspaston]"; ease grows "as the number of pulleys through which the rope runs is increased"; and, in Cohen and Drabkin's rendering, "the more 'limbs' [kola] the rope is bent into, the easier the weight will be moved." Book III.2-5 describes cranes with one, two, three and four masts, the single mast stayed by "three or four ropes ... fastened to its top." [P via Nix and Cohen-Drabkin]
- Vitruvius X.2 (Morgan). Two timbers "fastened together at the upper end by a bolt, then spread apart at the bottom"; at the top "a block, which some call a 'rechamus'" with two sheaves; the rope "carried over the sheave at the top, then let fall and passed round a sheave in a block below"; a windlass "turned round by working the handspikes"; iron shears at the bottom block whose prongs grip holes in the stone. "This kind of machinery, revolving with three sheaves, is called a trispast"; with two below and three above, "a pentaspast." For heavier loads an axle "carrying on its centre a large drum, which some term a wheel," which men can tread. The third type, a single mast stayed on four sides, with blocks of three rows of sheaves and three traction ropes, pulled by three rows of men without a capstan, "is called a polyspast, because of the many revolving sheaves." All are "suited ... also to the loading and unloading of ships." Latin: rechamus (block), orbiculi (sheaves), sucula (windlass), forfices (shears). [P]
- Capacity. Estimates from Vitruvius (Lancaster 1999): the trispastos lets one man at 50 kg lift about 150 kg; the pentaspastos about 250; the polyspastos with four men at a winch about 3000 kg, and with a treadwheel about 6000 kg at half the crew. Coulton's own ceiling for a winch and six-sheave tackle: "10 x 6 x 100 kg = 6 tons," and with Vitruvius's gearing "weights of 20-30 tons could be lifted." Heaviest attested Roman lifts: the 53-ton capital block of Trajan's Column raised to 34 m; Baalbek architraves of up to 60 tons at 19 m, done with lifting towers and several capstans, not one crane. The 800-ton Baalbek trilithon stones were moved on the ground, never lifted by pulleys. Vitruvius's polyspastos sheave count is read as 6 per block (Coulton) or 15 in all (Wikipedia); flag it if quoting either. [S; W]
- The Haterii relief. A marble relief from the tomb of a family of Roman building contractors on the Via Labicana (found 1848; Vatican, Museo Gregoriano Profano inv. 9997-9998), late 1st or early 2nd century AD: a treadwheel crane with five men in the wheel beside the family's finished tomb. The builders advertised the machine that made them. [W: Vatican Museums; date disputed]

## Medieval

- Treadwheel cranes return to the record in France about 1225 (magna rota) and in harbours at Utrecht (1244), Antwerp (1263), Bruges (1288) and Hamburg (1291). Cathedral cranes climbed with the building and sometimes stayed in the roof. [W citing Matheus 1996]
- Bruges: the harbour crane on the Kraanplein, documented 1288, rebuilt 1434, in service until 1767, with two treadwheels trodden by the kraankinderen, the "crane children," to lift wine and beer barrels; it stands behind the wine merchant Jan van Eyewerve in Pieter Pourbus's portrait of 1551. [W: Musea Brugge; U for the 1269 Damme date]
- Villard de Honnecourt's portfolio (BnF fr. 19093, c. 1225-1235) draws lifting devices and a screw jack; folio numbers for the hoist are commonly given as 22v-23r. [W; U for folios]
- Ships: the block, a sheave in a wooden shell, is the sailor's pulley, and tackles rove through blocks handled every yard and anchor. [W]
- Crossbows: from the 14th century the windlass spanner used a pair of small pulley blocks and cords wound on a crank drum; the cranequin is rack and pinion, not a pulley. [W]
- Arabic sources: Qusta ibn Luqa's translation of Heron carried the pulley theory east; the Banu Musa and al-Jazari use pulleys as guides for counterweights in clocks rather than as force multipliers. [W]

## Renaissance

- Brunelleschi's ox hoist (Florence, c. 1420). The dome was built 1420-1436 and needed some 37,000 tons of material lifted, including over four million bricks (Ross King). The hoist was driven by oxen walking a circle; a screw and two crown wheels let the driver reverse the direction of the winch without unhitching the oxen, and three drums of different diameter gave three speeds. Leonardo drew it in the Codex Atlanticus, f. 1083v; Taccola and Bonaccorso Ghiberti also recorded it. The "castello" was a separate revolving crane on the dome. Standard study: Prager and Scaglia, Brunelleschi: Studies of His Technology and Inventions (1970). [W: Florence Cathedral; Museo Leonardiano; U for Prager and Scaglia]
- Taccola, De ingeneis (1419-1433) and De machinis (1449), records Brunelleschi's "lifting devices and reversible-gear systems." [W]
- Leonardo, Codex Madrid I, f. 36v: a block of 33 sheaves, 17 fixed and 16 on a moving beam, one rope through all, with a small counterweight, which he calls "quite sure, and the most suitable for regulating and prolonging the descent of the weight in a clock"; f. 25r: a tensioner with rings of fixed and movable pulleys. No verified quotation of Leonardo's own words on pulleys multiplying force. [W: Museo Leonardiano; U]
- Guidobaldo del Monte, Mechanicorum liber (Pesaro 1577), De trochlea, 28 propositions, the longest section of the book. He reduces every pulley arrangement to a lever "by an ingenious method": prove the proposition for a lever model, then transfer it to the real block, up to six sheaves. Proposition 28, corollary 2: "the more easily a power can move a weight, the more slowly it does so." Drake singled out this section as the one Galileo adopted. [S: Renn and Damerow 2010; Laird 2013; Rose, DSB]
- Galileo, Le Mecaniche, "Delle taglie" (c. 1600): the fixed pulley gives no advantage; the movable pulley halves the force; the advantage is the number of strands; and a jab at Aristotle, "quanto puerilmente s'ingannasse Aristotile, il quale stimò che, col far maggiore la girella ABC, si potesse con manco fatica levare il peso" (how childishly Aristotle deceived himself, thinking that by making the sheave larger one could lift the weight with less effort). The size of the sheave is irrelevant; only the strands count. [P]
- Stevin, De Beghinselen der Weeghconst (1586), uses a pulley in a corollary on oblique pulls; the pulley treatise proper, "vant catrolwicht," is in the Byvough of the Wisconstighe Gedachtenissen (1605), where the path rule above and the halves, thirds and quarters appear. Dijksterhuis stresses that Stevin "explicitly rejected the principle of virtual displacements as a theoretical foundation" even though he used the rule. [P: DBNL; S: Dijksterhuis]
- Ramelli, Le diverse et artificiose machine (1588): 10 of the 195 plates are cranes. Plate 169, a crane for lifting heavy weights with spur gear, lantern and lewis, "especially useful for lifting stones, mortar, and other construction material"; plate 181, a machine for moving heavy weights with hand crank, spur gear and lantern, worm and worm gear, where "the machine's pulleys are enlarged and featured in the background." [S: Science History Institute; Smithsonian]
- Besson, Theatrum instrumentorum (1578): "hauling machines, cranes, elevators, pumps, salvage machines" among 60 plates; specific pulley-crane plate numbers not verified. [W; U]

## The key insight and who had it

- Pseudo-Aristotle (c. 300 BC): more pulleys, less force; no rule.
- Heron (1st century AD): the more times the rope is bent, the easier; the pulley as a power reducible to the balance.
- Guidobaldo (1577): every block reduces to a lever; the exact rule for any arrangement, and "the more easily, the more slowly."
- Galileo (c. 1600): count the strands, ignore the sheave size, and force times path is conserved.
- Stevin (1605): the rule stated for all machines at once, path against force, which Mach called the seed of virtual work.

## Story beats

- A king asks for proof and gets a ship. Hiero wants "some great weight moved by a slight force"; Archimedes picks a three-master that took a crowd to drag ashore, loads it with people and cargo, sits down, and hauls it in by hand "as though she were gliding through the water." Hiero orders siege engines on the spot. [P]
- The crane that shrank the stones. Around 515 BC Greek builders got cranes, and within a generation nobody cut a block over twenty tons again. [S]
- A family of Roman contractors carves a working treadwheel crane, men inside the wheel, on their own tomb. [W]
- An ox walks in a circle in Florence for sixteen years, and a screw and two crown wheels let one driver send the load up or down without turning the ox round. [W]
- Bruges, 1288 to 1767: five centuries of "crane children" treading a wheel on the Kraanplein to lift wine barrels, painted behind a merchant in 1551. [W]
- Otis, 30 May 1854, New York Crystal Palace: he rides his hoist platform up, has the rope cut with an axe, and the safety catch holds. The first passenger elevator follows in 1857. [W]

## Descendants

- Flagpoles, blinds, clotheslines, well hoists: fixed pulleys, direction change only.
- Cranes: modern hook blocks are multi-sheave tackles on wire rope, the heirs of Vitruvius's rechamus.
- Elevators: Otis's safety of 1852-54; traction elevators run the rope over a driven sheave against a counterweight.
- Sailing rigging: blocks and tackles by the hundred, with the names above.
- Gym cable machines, rescue and climbing hauling systems (the "3:1" is a luff tackle built from pulleys and a progress-capture device), cable cars (Hallidie's Clay Street line, San Francisco, first run 2 August 1873, an endless cable over sheaves under the street). [W; U for gym and climbing]
- The differential chain hoist in every workshop.
- Belt pulleys and timing belts: the same word for a different machine. A belt over two wheels transmits rotation and changes speed; that is the wheel and axle, not the pulley of this file. The first mass-produced timing belt was in the 1962 Glas 1004. [W]

## Misconceptions to avoid

- "Count the pulleys." Count the strands at the moving block. A single-sheave movable block gives 2; a fixed double block gives 1.
- "A fixed pulley makes lifting easier." It gives advantage 1; what changes is the direction and the chance to use body weight.
- "Archimedes lifted a ship with a pulley." He hauled it along the beach with a compound pulley, and the "one pulley" reading is a translation artefact.
- "Bigger sheaves help." Galileo's "puerilmente" is aimed at exactly this.
- "The Egyptians built the pyramids with pulleys." No pulley evidence exists before the 9th century BC and no compound pulley before the Greeks.
- "The Romans lifted the Baalbek trilithon by crane." They moved it on the ground.

## Numbers for the illustrator and the physics stage

- Gun tackle (2 strands): 50 kg of pull holds 100 kg; pull 2 m of rope to lift 1 m.
- Luff tackle (3): 50 kg holds 150 kg; 3 m of rope per metre.
- Threefold purchase (6): 50 kg holds 300 kg ideally; with 5 percent lost per sheave the real figure is nearer 230 kg, and 6 m of rope per metre of lift.
- Vitruvius's trispastos, one man at 50 kgf: about 150 kg; pentaspastos: about 250 kg; polyspastos with four men on a windlass: about 3000 kg; with a treadwheel, 6000 kg (Lancaster's figures). [S via W]
- Plutarch's ship: a small merchantman of, say, 50 tons on greased timbers with a friction coefficient of 0.1 needs about 5 tonne-force to move; a seated man can pull about 50 kgf steadily; so the tackle would need an advantage near 100, which is a winch plus a large tackle, and a very long rope. The story is a story. [derived]
