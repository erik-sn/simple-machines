# Simple machines: the research dossier

This folder is the historical and physical groundwork for the site. The prose on the site will be placard-length; these files are the deep bench behind it. Every claim carries a reliability tag and a source, and dates that scholars dispute are marked as such. Nothing here should be quoted on the site without checking the tag.

Files:

- README.md (this file): what a simple machine is, how the canon of six formed, the overall arc, and a dated timeline.
- lever.md, wheel-and-axle.md, pulley.md, inclined-plane.md, wedge.md, screw.md: one file per machine, each with the physics, the evidence, the Renaissance treatment, the key insight, story beats, descendants and misconceptions.
- compound-machines.md: how the machines combine, historical compound machines, and what the Renaissance authors said about combining them.
- sources.md: annotated bibliography with URLs and reliability notes.

Reliability tags used throughout: [P] primary source seen in text or translation, [S] scholarly secondary source, [W] Wikipedia-level or reference-work level, [U] unverified or recalled and to be checked before use, [derived] a calculation done for this dossier.

## What a simple machine is

- The modern textbook definition: a device that changes the direction or magnitude of a force, so that a small force acting through a long distance does the same work as a large force acting through a short one. The ratio of the two forces is the mechanical advantage. [W]
- The idea has a Greek name before it has a Greek definition. Heron of Alexandria (1st century AD) calls the five devices "powers" (dynameis) "by which a given weight is moved by a given force," and says they "are all derived from one natural principle, though they are very different in form." [P via Nix and Cohen-Drabkin]
- Vitruvius, a generation earlier, defines a machine as "a combination of timbers fastened together, chiefly efficacious in moving great weights" (De architectura X.1.1) and says all machinery "is derived from nature, and is founded on the teaching and instruction of the revolution of the firmament," that is, on circular motion. [P: Morgan]
- The word: Greek mechane (Doric machana), a contrivance or device, from mechos, a means or expedient; Latin machina; the family includes "mechanic" and "machination." [W]
- Franz Reuleaux (Kinematics of Machinery, 1875, English 1876) replaced the list of six with kinematic pairs and chains, noting that levers, pulleys and wheels are rotary devices while planes, wedges and screws are sliding ones. The six survive as a teaching canon, not as an engineering taxonomy. [W]

## The one idea

Every machine in the list trades force for distance and nothing more. The Greeks stated it dynamically (the far end of a lever moves on a bigger circle), Archimedes proved it statically for the lever, Jordanus proved the lever and the ramp from what one weight can lift another only by falling farther, Stevin proved the ramp by refusing to believe in perpetual motion, and Galileo said it flatly around 1600: nature's rule is "that no resistance can be overcome by a force that is not more powerful than it," and the force in every machine runs its course as many times as the load outweighs it. Two centuries later Lagrange made that statement, the principle of virtual velocities, the foundation of all statics. The site's through-line is that no machine gives work for free; the history is the story of how long it took to say so.

## The arc in eleven steps

### 1. Tools before theory

Digging sticks, wedge-shaped hand axes (Oldowan tools from 2.6 million years ago, Acheulean hand axes from about 1.7 million), balance beams, shadufs (Mesopotamia by c. 2200 BC), ramps (Hatnub quarry, c. 2560 BC), potter's wheels and cart wheels (4th millennium BC), rope over a beam. Every machine on the list was in daily use two thousand years before anyone wrote down why it worked. See the machine files for the archaeology and its uncertainties.

### 2. The Greek question

The Mechanical Problems attributed to Aristotle (c. 300 BC, a Peripatetic author, identity disputed) opens by saying that some remarkable things happen by nature and others "contrary to nature, which are produced by skill for the benefit of mankind," and that "the original cause of all such phenomena is the circle." The balance is reduced to the circle, the lever to the balance, and nearly everything else to the lever. Its 35 problems treat the lever (3), the oar (4), the windlass (13), the wedge (17) and the pulley (18) but not the inclined plane. [P: Hett, Loeb 1936] The Renaissance read it avidly: Greek in the Aldine Aristotle (Venice 1495-98); Latin by Vettor Fausto (Paris 1517) and Leonico Tomeo (1525); Piccolomini's paraphrase (Rome 1547); Monantheuil's Greek and Latin (Paris 1599); Baldi's Archimedean commentary (Mainz 1621). Guidobaldo pointedly chose Heron's five powers over the Aristotelian problems as the frame of his book. [S: Nenci 2011; Ferreiro 2009]

### 3. Archimedes (c. 287-212 BC)

- Proved the law of the lever in On the Equilibrium of Planes I, propositions 6-7, from the postulate that "equal weights at equal distances are in equilibrium." [P via Heath]
- The boast. Pappus, Collection VIII, proposition 10 (c. AD 320; Hultsch pp. 1060-61), in Koine Greek: "dos moi pou sto kai kino ten gen," give me where I may stand, and I move the earth. It is said there in praise of Heron's baroulkos gear train, not of a lever. Simplicius (6th century) has a Doric version; Tzetzes (12th century) is the only source that names an instrument, the charistion, a lever-balance. The Doric form printed on Wikiquote and Wikipedia is a modern hybrid, and "give me a lever" is a modern splice. Plutarch (Marcellus 14.7) instead reports the claim that "with any given force it was possible to move any given weight," and that "if there were another world, and he could go to it, he could move this." [P: Hultsch's Latin column; S for Simplicius and Tzetzes; P for Plutarch]
- The ship. Plutarch, Marcellus 14.8: at Hiero's request Archimedes loaded a beached three-masted merchantman with cargo and passengers, sat down at a distance, and "quietly setting in motion with his hand a system of compound pulleys, drew her towards him smoothly and evenly, as though she were gliding through the water." Athenaeus (Deipnosophistae V.207a-b, quoting Moschion) tells a different story: Archimedes launched Hiero's giant ship, the Syracusia, into the sea with a "helix," which translators have rendered as windlass or screw, and its bilge "although of a most enormous depth, was pumped out by one man, by means of the screw, an engine which was the contrivance of Archimedes." Plutarch wrote three centuries after the event and Athenaeus four; Moschion is lost and his date unknown. Both are good stories and neither is evidence of the mechanism. See pulley.md and screw.md. [P: Perrin; Yonge]
- The claw. Polybius VIII.5-6 (the earliest source, writing within living memory of the siege of 213-212 BC, or 214-212 by Livy's book division) describes beams swung out over the wall that "let down an iron hand attached to a chain" which "would clutch at the ship, and when he had got hold of her by the prow, would press down the opposite end," lifting the bow and dropping it. Livy XXIV.34 has "an iron grapnel, fastened to a stout chain ... thrown on to the bow by means of a swing-beam projecting over the wall." Plutarch, Marcellus 15, has ships "seized by iron claws, or beaks like the beaks of cranes, drawn straight up into the air." Livy adds the lead counterweight. The three accounts agree on a beam over the wall, a chain-hung grapnel and a sudden release; Rorres and Harris (2001) reconstruct a counterweighted lever, not a pulley crane, and warn that existing translations are "extremely misleading in places." A 2005 television experiment showed a design could capsize a model ship; that shows possibility, not history. [P: Paton; Livy; Perrin; S: Rorres and Harris; W for the test]
- The death. Livy (XXV.31) says a Roman soldier killed him, "intent upon figures which he had traced in the dust," not knowing who he was; Plutarch (Marcellus 19) gives three versions, one with a demonstration he would not leave unfinished. "Noli turbare circulos meos" is in no ancient source; the oldest form is Valerius Maximus VIII.7.ext.7 (c. AD 30), "noli, obsecro, istum disturbare," do not, I beg, disturb that; Elias (6th century) has him say "at my head, not at my line"; Tzetzes and Zonaras (12th century) have "stand away from my diagram." [P: Valerius Maximus; S: Rorres's NYU pages]
- Marcellus's joke, Plutarch 17.1: "Let us stop fighting against this geometrical Briareus, who uses our ships like cups to ladle water from the sea." Polybius VIII.7 has the original: "Archimedes uses my ships to ladle sea-water into his wine cups, but my sambuca band is flogged out of the banquet in disgrace." [P]
- Archimedes and the machine as an unworthy subject: Plutarch 17.3-4 says he regarded "the work of an engineer and every art that ministers to the needs of life as ignoble and vulgar" and "would not consent to leave behind him any treatise on this subject"; his tomb was to carry a sphere in a cylinder. Pappus reports he wrote only On Sphere-Making among mechanical works, yet Pappus cites his On Balances and Heron names him about ten times, including a "Book of Supports" on the loads carried by pillars under a beam. Vitruvius (VII praef. 14) lists him among the writers on machines with Diades, Archytas, Ctesibius and Philo. [P: Plutarch; Vitruvius; S: Heath II; Schiefsky]
- The screw pump. Diodorus Siculus (1st century BC) says the Nile delta is irrigated "by means of a contrivance which was invented by Archimedes of Syracuse and is called, after its shape, a screw" (I.34) and that Spanish silver mines are drained with "the Egyptian screw, which was invented by Archimedes of Syracuse at the time of his visit to Egypt" (V.37). The screw may predate him; Vitruvius, Philo and Strabo describe it without naming him. Stephanie Dalley's argument that Sennacherib's Nineveh gardens (c. 700 BC) used screws is contested. See screw.md. [P: Oldfather; W]

### 4. Heron of Alexandria and the five powers

- Date: fixed by a lunar eclipse in his Dioptra that Neugebauer matched to 13 March AD 62; so he flourished around AD 60-70. Earlier guesses ranged from 150 BC to AD 250. [S; W]
- The Mechanics (Mechanika, also cited as Baroulkos, "weight-lifter") is in three books. Book I: the baroulkos, a geared winch by which a force of 5 talents moves 1000 talents; motion, centres of gravity, the Archimedean principles of weighing. Book II: the five powers, first their form, use and names (II.1-6), then the concentric circles and the theory of each power (II.7-20), then combinations of the powers and the three ways to move 1000 talents with 5, by wheels, by pulleys, by levers (II.21-32), then a set of "why" questions in the manner of the Mechanical Problems (II.33-34), and centres of gravity of polygons (II.35-41). Book III: cranes, presses (including screw presses with a female screw), and how to cut a screw thread. [S: Heath II, pp. 346-352; Drachmann 1963]
- The list, II.1, in Schiefsky's translation from the Arabic: "Since the powers by which a given weight is moved by a given force are five, we necessarily have to explain their forms, their uses, and their names, because these powers are all reduced to the same nature, though they are very different in form." The names: "the axle-in-wheel, the lever, the pulley, the wedge, and what is called the 'endless' screw." Heron reduces all five to the balance and the circle (II.7-20). The inclined plane is absent; his attempt at it (I.23) is wrong. Pappus's proposition 10 lists the same five in another order (wedge, lever, screw, pulley, axle-in-wheel), so the order is not canonical. [P via Nix; S: Schiefsky 2008]
- Transmission. The Greek is lost except for fragments in Pappus. The whole survives in an Arabic translation by Qusta ibn Luqa of Baalbek (c. 820-912), a Melkite Christian scholar working in Abbasid Baghdad, under the title "On lifting heavy objects"; one recent study dates the translation to 862-866; manuscripts in Leiden, the British Library (Add. 23390) and Manchester. Heron's Mechanics bypassed the Latin Middle Ages entirely and re-entered Europe only with Carra de Vaux in 1893; the Renaissance knew its five powers through Pappus. First modern edition: Carra de Vaux, Les mécaniques ou l'élévateur de Héron d'Alexandrie (Paris 1893, with French). Standard edition: L. Nix and W. Schmidt, Heronis Alexandrini Opera II (Leipzig 1900, with German). Standard study: A. G. Drachmann, The Mechanical Technology of Greek and Roman Antiquity (Copenhagen and Madison 1963). There is still no complete English translation. [S; W; the exact date of the translation is U]

### 5. Vitruvius, the Roman handbook (c. 30-20 BC)

- Book X of De architectura, dedicated to Augustus, is the only ancient treatise on building to survive. X.1: the definition of a machine and its three kinds, climbing (scansorium), pneumatic (spiritale) and hauling (tractorium), and the difference between a machine, worked by many, and an engine (organum), worked by one. X.2: hoisting machines, the trispastos with three sheaves, the pentaspastos with five, the windlass (sucula), the treadwheel drum, the polyspastos, and the anecdote of Chersiphron and Metagenes rolling the column shafts and architraves of the temple at Ephesus inside wooden wheels. X.3: "the elements of all machinery" are rectilinear and circular motion; the lever with its fulcrum (hypomochlion), the steelyard, the oar, the rudder, the yoke. X.6: the water screw, built on a beam sixteen times as long as it is thick, with eight helical channels of pitch-coated willow, set at the slope of a 3-4-5 triangle and trodden by men. [P: Morgan 1914]
- Editions: the oldest manuscript (BL Harley 2767) is Carolingian, c. 800-825; Poggio Bracciolini drew attention to a copy at St Gall in 1416; first printed at Rome c. 1486 (Sulpicius); first illustrated by Fra Giocondo at Venice in 1511; first vernacular (Italian) by Cesariano at Como in 1521; Daniele Barbaro's commentary with Palladio's illustrations, Venice 1556. Vitruvius was the Renaissance engineer's textbook. [W]

### 6. Pappus and the summary of Greek mechanics (c. AD 320)

- Pappus of Alexandria is dated by a solar eclipse he computed for 18 October 320. Book VIII of his Collection (Synagoge) is the last ancient treatise on mechanics. Its preface defines mechanics as having a theoretical part (geometry, arithmetic, astronomy, physics) and a practical part (metalworking, building, carpentry, painting), following Heron, and lists the branches: the makers of "mechanical powers" who lift great weights with small force, the makers of war engines, of water-lifting machines, of automata and wonder-working devices, of sphere-models. He says Archimedes alone understood the cause of all of them. [S: Heath II, pp. 427-430]
- Then the five powers, in Heron's order, and proposition 10: "to move a given weight by a given force," solved with Heron's baroulkos, a train of toothed wheels driven by an endless screw. Proposition 9 is the inclined plane, solved wrongly (see inclined-plane.md). Book VIII also contains the "give me a place to stand" line and instructions for cutting screw threads and toothed wheels. [S: Heath II, pp. 430-434]
- Federico Commandino's Latin translation, Mathematicae collectiones (Pesaro 1588, posthumous, seen through the press by his pupil Guidobaldo), put all this into the hands of the Urbino school and, through Guidobaldo, Galileo. Guidobaldo's preface follows Pappus "as leader" and says everything in machines "must be referred to those five machines which are endowed with infinite force." [S: Palmieri 2008; Laird 2013]

### 7. The Islamic and medieval bridge (9th to 13th century)

- Baghdad, 9th century. Qusta ibn Luqa translates Heron's Mechanics. Thabit ibn Qurra (836-901) writes the Book of the Steelyard (Kitab al-qarastun), the founding text of the Arabic science of weights, which reaches Latin as Liber karastonis through Gerard of Cremona in 12th-century Toledo. The three Banu Musa brothers (Ahmad, Muhammad and al-Hasan ibn Shakir) write the Book of Ingenious Devices (Kitab al-hiyal, c. 850) for the House of Wisdom: about a hundred trick vessels, fountains and automata drawing on Heron and Philo, notable for self-regulating valves and delay mechanisms; English by D. R. Hill, 1979. [S: MacTutor; Hill; W]
- Al-Andalus, 11th century. Ibn Khalaf al-Muradi's Book of Secrets in the Results of Thoughts (Kitab al-asrar fi nataij al-afkar) survives in a single manuscript copied in 1266, now in the Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana in Florence: clocks, automata and water-raising machines with segmental and epicyclic gears, the earliest known complex gearing in the Islamic west. Facsimile with digital reconstructions by Leonardo3, 2008. [W; S]
- Diyarbakir, 1206. Ismail al-Jazari (1136-1206) finishes the Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices for the Artuqid court: fifty devices in six categories, with construction instructions, including a crank-driven water pump, a double-acting reciprocating pump with suction and valves, segmental gears, a camshaft, the elephant clock and the castle clock. English by D. R. Hill, 1974. [W; S: Hill]
- Latin Europe, 13th century. Jordanus de Nemore's science of weights (Elementa super demonstrationem ponderum; De ratione ponderis) proves the lever from work and the inclined plane correctly, using "positional gravity" and virtual displacements. Villard de Honnecourt's portfolio (c. 1230) sketches hoists and a water-powered saw. The treadwheel crane reappears in France c. 1225 and in harbours from 1244 (Utrecht), 1263 (Antwerp), 1288 (Bruges). [S: DSB; W]

### 8. The Renaissance theatre of machines (1430-1620)

The engineers drew before the mathematicians proved. In order:

- Mariano di Jacopo, called Taccola, "the Sienese Archimedes" (1382-c. 1453): De ingeneis (begun c. 1419, completed 1433) and De machinis (1449), hoists, mills, pumps and siege engines, with the first European drawing of a piston pump and records of Brunelleschi's dome machines. Manuscripts rediscovered in Munich and Florence in the 1960s. [W]
- Francesco di Giorgio Martini (1439-1501): Trattato di architettura, ingegneria e arte militare (completed after 1482, circulated in manuscript), with column-lifting machines, screw jacks, cranes, mills and pumps. Leonardo owned and annotated a copy (Laurenziana, Ashburnham 361). [W]
- Leonardo da Vinci (1452-1519): the Codex Madrid I (Biblioteca Nacional, Ms. 8937, 192 folios, 1490s), rediscovered in 1965, is an "elements of machines": gears, screws, springs, bearings, friction, levers. Its first modern publication was Ladislao Reti's facsimile of 1974. Leonardo measured sliding friction around 1493 and left it unpublished; Amontons restated the laws in 1699 and Coulomb refined them in 1785. [W; S: Reti]
- Georgius Agricola (1494-1555): De re metallica (Basel, Froben, 1556, posthumous), twelve books and 292 woodcuts; Book V on breaking rock with "iron wedges, hammers and crowbars" or with fire; Book VI on windlasses, horse whims, treadwheels, bucket chains, piston pumps and ventilation. English by Herbert and Lou Henry Hoover, 1912. [W; P: Gutenberg 38015]
- Jacques Besson (c. 1540-1573): Theatrum instrumentorum et machinarum, first issued at Orleans or Lyon c. 1571-72 with plates by Androuet du Cerceau and no text, then Lyon 1578 with descriptions by Beroalde de Verville and new engravings by Rene Boyvin; 60 plates; includes a screw-cutting lathe. Besson was appointed "master of the King's Engines" by Charles IX in 1569. [W]
- Agostino Ramelli (1531-c. 1610): Le diverse et artificiose machine del Capitano Agostino Ramelli (Paris 1588, self-published, Italian and French in parallel), 195 engraved plates, over a hundred of them water-raising machines, plus mills, bridges, cranes, siege engines and the reading wheel (plate CLXXXVIII). Ramelli had breached a bastion at the siege of La Rochelle in 1572-73 and dedicated the book to Henri III. English by Gnudi and Ferguson, 1976. [W]
- Vittorio Zonca (1568-1603): Novo teatro di machine et edificii (Padua 1607, posthumous, publisher Bertelli), with the silk-throwing mill, rolling mill, paper mill and presses; some plates were redrawn in China by Johann Schreck in 1627. [W]
- Fausto Veranzio (Vrancic): Machinae novae (Venice, c. 1615-16), with the "homo volans" parachute plate. [W; date U]
- Guidobaldo del Monte's 1577 book (below) is the hinge between this world and the next: he drew real machines and proved theorems about them.

### 9. The mechanicians, 1546-1621

- Niccolo Tartaglia, Quesiti et inventioni diverse (Venice 1546), Book VIII on the science of weights, reworks Jordanus; the full De ratione ponderis is printed from his papers in 1565. [S]
- Girolamo Cardano, De subtilitate (1550) and Opus novum de proportionibus (1570), treats the machines and gets the inclined plane wrong (force proportional to angle). [W/S]
- Federico Commandino (1509-1575) translates Archimedes and Pappus; Pappus appears at Pesaro in 1588. [W]
- Guidobaldo del Monte (1545-1607), Mechanicorum liber (Pesaro 1577; Italian by Filippo Pigafetta as Le mechaniche, Venice 1581): six treatises, De libra, De vecte, De trochlea, De axe in peritrochio, De cuneo, De cochlea, in Euclidean form with definitions, axioms, postulates and propositions. Every machine is reduced to the lever and the lever to the balance, the wedge to inclined planes and levers, the screw to a wound wedge. He rejects Jordanus and Tartaglia for "mistaking effects for causes," and rejects the idea that a machine can move a weight without a force exceeding it: for sustaining a weight there is one exact rule; for moving it "one cannot give a firm rule." He got Galileo his chairs at Pisa (1589) and Padua (1592). [S: Renn and Damerow 2010; Laird 2013]
- Giovanni Battista Benedetti, Diversarum speculationum (Turin 1585), measures a force's effect by the perpendicular from the pivot to its line of action. Guidobaldo's copy has "empty and false" in the margin. [S: Renn and Damerow 2012]
- Simon Stevin (1548-1620), De Beghinselen der Weeghconst (Leiden 1586, with De Weeghdaet and De Beghinselen des Waterwichts, dedicated to Rudolf II in August 1586): the clootcrans proof of the inclined plane and the parallelogram of forces. Motto: "Wonder en is gheen wonder." The general rule for pulleys in terms of path, "as the path of the doer is to the path of the sufferer, so is the force of the sufferer to the force of the doer," belongs to the Byvough of the Wisconstighe Gedachtenissen (1605-08), not to 1586, and Dijksterhuis notes that Stevin used it while rejecting virtual displacements as a foundation. [P: DBNL text; S: Dijksterhuis]
- Galileo Galilei (1564-1642), Le Mecaniche (Padua, drafts from 1593, definitive text c. 1598-1600; circulated in manuscript; printed in Mersenne's French translation, Les mechaniques de Galilee, Paris 1634; Italian first printed by Danesi, Ravenna 1649; Favaro's edition in the Edizione Nazionale vol. II, 1891; English by Stillman Drake in On Motion and On Mechanics, Madison 1960). Chapters: the utilities of mechanics; definitions (including "momento"); some warnings; the steelyard and lever; the axle in the wheel and the capstan; pulleys; the screw; Archimedes' water screw; the force of percussion. The opening chapter says engineers "deceive themselves" who think a machine lets a small force move a great weight, because nature's constitution is "che niuna resistenza possa essere superata da forza, che di quella non sia più potente"; the four things to consider are weight, force, distance and time; the utility of machines is to move a weight "tutto insieme," all at once, to work where a man cannot, and to use cheap or inanimate power. [P: Favaro edition on it.wikisource; W for printing history]
- Bernardino Baldi, In mechanica Aristotelis problemata exercitationes (Mainz 1621, posthumous), the last great commentary on the Mechanical Problems. [S: Nenci 2011]

### 10. How the list became six

- Heron and Pappus: five (wheel and axle, lever, pulley, wedge, screw). Guidobaldo (1577): the balance plus the five, in six treatises. Galileo (c. 1600): lever and steelyard, axle and capstan, pulleys, screw with the inclined plane derived inside it, and the water screw; no wedge chapter. [P/S]
- Descartes to Constantijn Huygens, 5 October 1637, "Explication des engins": "the invention of all these engines is founded on only one principle," that the force which lifts 100 pounds two feet lifts 200 pounds one foot; and the engines "are the pulley, the inclined plane, the wedge, the wheel, the screw, the lever, and some others. For if one does not want to refer them to one another one can count more, and if one does, one has no need of so many." The earliest six-with-inclined-plane list found, and it already calls the count conventional. [P: Adam-Tannery I, pp. 435-448]
- John Wilkins, Mathematicall Magick (London 1648), Book I "Archimedes," ch. 2: the mechanical faculties "are usually reckoned to be these six: 1. Libra, the Ballance. 2. Vectis, the Leaver. 3. Axis in Peritrochio, the Wheel. 4. Trochlea, the Pulley. 5. Cuneus, the Wedge. 6. Cochlea, the Screw." Six, with the balance, not the plane. [P]
- 's Gravesande, Physices elementa mathematica (1720; English 1747): five simple machines, lever, axis in peritrochio, pulley, wedge and screw, with the balance separate and the inclined plane among "oblique powers." Desaguliers, A Course of Experimental Philosophy I (1734): seven, "the Balance, the Leaver, the Pulley, the Axis in Peritrochio, the Inclined Plane, the Wedge, and the Screw," with the note that "Authors differ in their Enumeration of the Mechanical Powers, some making them to be Six, excluding the Inclined Plane ... Others have reduced them to Five, considering that the Screw is only a Wedge carried round a Cylinder: And others again have made the Leaver and Balance to be the same Power." By 1734 the count was openly a convention. [P: archive.org OCR]
- The modern six, balance dropped and plane admitted, is in print by the end of the century: Hutton's Mathematical and Philosophical Dictionary (1795-96), "These simple machines are usually accounted six in number, viz, the Lever, the Wheel and Axle, or Axis in Peritrochio, the Pulley, the Inclined Plane, the Wedge, and the Screw"; the Encyclopaedia Britannica of 1773 ("Mechanics," vol. 3, p. 44) is cited by Wikipedia for the same list; the 7th edition (1842) says the elementary machines "have been generally reckoned six in number" and adds that they reduce to three: lever, inclined plane, and rope. [P: Hutton; EB 1842; W: EB 1773]
- So: Wikipedia's statement that "Renaissance scientists" defined the six is unsupported. The Renaissance canon was the balance plus Heron's five; the plane entered through Stevin, Galileo and Descartes; the count was conventional through the 18th century; the textbook six is a late-18th-century settlement whose first author is not identified. Treat any attribution to Hooke, Wallis or another named author as [U].
- The term "mechanical powers" for the six is standard English from the 17th to the early 20th century; the phrase "mechanical advantage" does not occur in Wilkins, 's Gravesande, Desaguliers or Mach, and Merriam-Webster's first recorded use is 1852. [P; W]

### 11. Virtual work and the formalism

- Descartes (letter to Huygens, 1637, above) states the force-times-height principle outright; Wallis (Mechanica, 1670) uses displacement arguments for the machines. [P for Descartes; U for Wallis's details]
- Pierre Varignon (1654-1722), Projet d'une nouvelle mécanique (Paris 1687): the composition of forces (parallelogram) applied to all the simple machines, so that "the law of the lever does not hold a privileged position in statics." Nouvelle mécanique ou statique (1725, posthumous). [S: DSB]
- Johann Bernoulli's letter to Varignon of 26 January 1717 states the principle of virtual velocities in general form; Varignon printed it in the 1725 book. Wikipedia's "1715" (from Capecchi) conflicts with the DSB, Mach and Lagrange's own "une de ses Lettres à Varignon, datée de 1717." [S: DSB; P: Lagrange]
- D'Alembert, Traité de dynamique (1743), extends it to dynamics. Lagrange, Mécanique analytique (Paris 1788; 2nd ed. 1811-15): "If any system whatsoever of bodies or points, each acted on by any forces, is in equilibrium, and one gives this system any small movement, in virtue of which each point traverses an infinitely small distance that expresses its virtual velocity, the sum of the forces, each multiplied by the distance its point of application traverses in the direction of that force, will always be equal to zero." He credits Bernoulli (1717) with seeing its generality and traces statics to the lever, the pulley and the inclined plane. His preface boasts that "no diagrams will be found in this work." [P: fr.wikisource]
- Mechanical advantage as taught now: ideal MA from geometry (ratio of distances moved), actual MA from measured forces, efficiency = actual / ideal. Ideal MA is the same statement as virtual work: input force times input distance equals output force times output distance. [W]

### 12. Today

- The 2016 Nobel Prize in Chemistry (Sauvage, Stoddart, Feringa) was awarded "for the design and synthesis of molecular machines": Sauvage's catenane (1983), Stoddart's rotaxane shuttle on an axle (1991), Feringa's unidirectional molecular motor (1999) and nanocar (2011). Cells have run rotary and walking machines for billions of years: ATP synthase is a turbine, kinesin walks along microtubules, the bacterial flagellum is a rotary motor. [W; Nobel press release]
- The screw is the most produced machine element in the world; the metrology of every machine tool rests on the lead screw (Maudslay c. 1800) and the standard thread (Whitworth 1841); ball screws position the tables of CNC machines. See screw.md. [S/W; check the screw file for tags]
- The plane, the wedge and the lever are inside every keyboard key, zip, knife and door handle. The point of the site: the six are not antiques.

## Where the sources disagree

- The shaduf: c. 3000 BC (Paipetis and Ceccarelli) against c. 2200 BC (the Akkadian seal). Use "by 2200 BC."
- The Roman steelyard: mid-4th century BC (Museo Galileo) against 1st century BC (Topoi project).
- Archimedes' ship: Plutarch's compound pulley against Athenaeus's helix; different ships, different devices.
- The siege of Syracuse: 214-212 or 213-212 BC.
- Qusta ibn Luqa's translation date: 9th century; no closer date verified.
- Zonca's death: 1602 or 1603.
- Bernoulli's letter: 1717 (DSB, Mach, Lagrange) against 1715 (Capecchi, via Wikipedia); 1717 is right.
- Agricola's woodcuts: 292 (Wikipedia) or 289 (Dover).
- Poggio's Vitruvius: 1416 (most) or 1414.
- The Banu Musa: "commissioned by al-Ma'mun" and "c. 850" cannot both be exact.
- Al-Jazari's court: Diyarbakir (Hill) or Mardin (Wikipedia).
- Haterii relief: late 1st century (art historians) or early 2nd (Vatican Museums).
- Cardano's inclined plane rule: 1550 or 1570.
- Who first listed the canonical six: no reliable attribution found.

## Things I could not verify

- Ramelli plate numbers beyond those verified in the machine files (155-156, 162-167, 169, 181, 188); the Gnudi and Ferguson edition should be checked before citing others.
- The claim that Guidobaldo said machines "cannot cheat nature": not found in Pigafetta's text or in Laird, Rose or Bertoloni Meli; the verified passage is Galileo's. Attribute it to Galileo only.
- Drake's exact English wording for the Galileo passages; the Italian is quoted from Favaro's text and the English renderings in these files are working translations.
- The Festa and Roux chapter on the inclined plane (2008): blocked to automated fetching; used through its abstract and through works that cite it.
- Specific Codex Madrid I folio numbers for the lever and pulley studies.
- The claim that Stevin's wreath is on his gravestone: no grave is known; treat as legend.
