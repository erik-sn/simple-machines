# The lever

The lever is the machine the theory was built on. Every other machine in the Greek list was explained by reducing it to a lever, and the lever was reduced to the balance, and the balance to the circle. It is also the machine with the oldest pedigree in use: a digging stick is a lever, and so is a shaduf, a balance beam, an oar, and a crowbar.

Reliability tags: [P] primary source seen in text or translation, [S] scholarly secondary, [W] Wikipedia-level, [U] unverified or recalled, check before use.

## Physics in plain terms

- Law of the lever: a lever balances when effort times effort arm equals load times load arm, F1 d1 = F2 d2 about the fulcrum. [textbook; Archimedes below]
- Ideal mechanical advantage: MA = effort arm / load arm. A crowbar with a 90 cm handle and a 10 cm tip gives 9. [textbook]
- The trade: the far end of a lever sweeps a bigger arc than the near end, so the effort moves farther than the load in exactly the ratio of the arms. Work in equals work out. Galileo's phrasing (Le Mecaniche, c. 1600): the force must be faster than the load "as many times as the weight exceeds the force." [P, see below]
- An ideal lever "does not dissipate or store energy": no friction at the pivot, no bending of the beam. Real levers lose a little to pivot friction and beam flex; efficiency is normally very high, which is why the lever was the first machine whose law was found exactly. [W]
- Classes, defined by what sits in the middle:
  - Class 1, fulcrum in the middle: seesaw, crowbar, scissors, pliers, balance, claw hammer pulling a nail. MA can be above or below 1.
  - Class 2, load in the middle: wheelbarrow, nutcracker, bottle opener, car brake pedal, bellows. MA is always above 1.
  - Class 3, effort in the middle: tweezers, tongs, fishing rod, hoe, swung hammer, the human forearm and jaw. MA is always below 1; the trade goes the other way, force for speed and reach. [W; Science North curriculum]
- A class 3 lever is not a bad lever. The forearm is one: the biceps pulls a few centimetres from the elbow and the hand at the far end moves fast and far, which is what a throwing arm needs.
- Compound levers chain the output of one lever into the input of the next; the advantages multiply. Nail clippers, a piano key mechanism and the three bones of the middle ear are compound levers. [W]

## Earliest evidence

- Balance scales. The oldest attested weighing equipment is Egyptian: deben balance weights from the reign of Sneferu, 4th Dynasty, c. 2600 BC (Petruso 1981). Indus cities used uniform polished stone cubes as weights c. 2400-1800 BC. Claims of "5000 BC" for the Egyptian balance rest on a limestone beam of possibly predynastic date in the Petrie collection and should be hedged. [W citing S; U for 5000 BC]
- Shaduf. The earliest Mesopotamian evidence is a cylinder seal of about 2200 BC, in the Akkadian period; one scholarly source pushes it to c. 3000 BC. In Egypt it appears after 2000 BC, probably in the 18th Dynasty; the famous painted shaduf is in the garden scene of the tomb of Ipuy (TT217) at Deir el-Medina, a sculptor under Ramesses II, c. 1279-1213 BC. Sources disagree by centuries; say "by 2200 BC in Mesopotamia." [W; Yannopoulos et al. 2015]
- Pyramid levers. Herodotus II.125: the stones were raised "with machines made of short pieces of timber," one per course or one machine carried from stage to stage. No such machine survives; Leonardo later drew a rocker reconstruction. Recesses and handling bosses on obelisks of over 100 tons show that the Egyptians levered them into position (Clarke and Engelbach). [P: Herodotus; S via W]
- The oar. Pseudo-Aristotle, Mechanical Problems 4, asks why rowers amidships contribute most and answers that the tholepin "is the fulcrum" and the loom is longest amidships. Vitruvius X.3.5-6 repeats the oar argument. [P]

## Antiquity

- Pseudo-Aristotle, Mechanical Problems (c. 300 BC; Peripatetic, author unknown). Opening: remarkable things happen "contrary to nature, which are produced by skill for the benefit of mankind," and "the original cause of all such phenomena is the circle." Then the chain: "the phenomena about the balance are reduced to the circle, those about the lever to the balance, and nearly all other mechanical motions to the lever." [P: Hett, Loeb 1936, 847a-848a]
- Problem 3, the lever: "Why is it that small forces can move great weights by means of a lever ... seeing that one naturally adds the weight of the lever?" Answer: the fulcrum is the centre, the two weights are at the ends, and "the ratio of the weight moved to the weight moving it is the inverse ratio of the distances from the centre"; the point farther from the centre "describes the greater circle." This is the law of the lever stated dynamically, by speeds, three generations before Archimedes proved it statically. [P: 850a-b]
- Archimedes, On the Equilibrium of Planes I (3rd century BC). Postulate 1: "Equal weights at equal distances are in equilibrium, and equal weights at unequal distances are not in equilibrium but incline towards the weight that is at the greater distance." Propositions 6 and 7: "Two magnitudes, whether commensurable or incommensurable, balance at distances reciprocally proportional to the magnitudes." The proof of 6 splits each weight into unit weights spread along the beam so that the row becomes symmetric about the fulcrum and postulate 1 applies. [P via Heath, The Works of Archimedes]
- Ernst Mach's complaint (The Science of Mechanics, 1883): the assumption that a weight's effect is measured by weight times distance "is more or less covertly or tacitly introduced by Archimedes." The proof presupposes what it proves. The point is fair and often made on placards; use with care. [P: Mach]
- The boast. Pappus, Collection VIII, proposition 10 (c. AD 320), Hultsch pp. 1060-61, in Koine Greek: "dos moi pou sto kai kino ten gen," give me where I may stand, and I move the earth; Hultsch's Latin, "da mihi, ubi consistam, et terram movebo." Pappus attaches it to Heron's baroulkos gear train, "this is the mechanical invention of Archimedes, exulting in which he is reported to have said ..." Simplicius (6th century, on Physics VII.5) gives a Doric form, "pa bo kai kino tan gan"; John Tzetzes (12th century, Chiliades II.130) is the only ancient author who names an instrument, the charistion, a lever-balance. The Doric "dos moi pa sto kai tan gan kinaso" printed on Wikiquote and Wikipedia is a modern hybrid, and "give me a lever and a place to stand" is a modern splice; no ancient text has both. Plutarch (Marcellus 14.7) gives instead: "if there were another world, and he could go to it, he could move this." [P: Hultsch's Latin column; S: Dijksterhuis for Simplicius; P: Plutarch, Perrin]
- Plutarch's demonstration was not a lever. Hiero asked for proof and Archimedes drew a beached, loaded three-master toward himself "without any great effort, but quietly setting in motion with his hand a system of compound pulleys ... as though she were gliding through the water." See pulley.md. [P: Plutarch, Marcellus 14.8]
- Heron of Alexandria, Mechanics II.1 (1st century AD; Greek lost, Arabic translation by Qusta ibn Luqa, 9th century): "the powers by which a given weight is moved by a given force are five," axle-in-wheel, lever, pulley, wedge, screw, and "these powers are all derived from one natural principle, though they are very different in form." Book II.2 treats the lever. Book I.32-33 gives the principles of weighing and cites Archimedes' lost book On Balances. [P via Nix and Cohen-Drabkin; S: Heath II]
- Vitruvius, De architectura X.3 (c. 30-20 BC). "An iron lever (vectis) applied to a load which a multitude of hands cannot move" is rested on a support "which the Greeks call hypomochlion" (Vitruvius's Latin is pressio), the tongue (lingula) under the load, and "one man's strength, bearing down upon the head of it, heaves up the weight." The proof he offers is the steelyard (statera), whose small sliding counterpoise (aequipondium), moved outward, "makes a small and inferior weight equal to a very heavy object." [P: Latin on LacusCurtius; Morgan translation]
- The steelyard. Museo Galileo dates its appearance to Campania around the mid 4th century BC; the Topoi project (Büttner and Renn) puts its inception "presumably in the first century BCE." Roman bronze examples from Pompeii (1st century AD) have lead-filled counterpoises shaped as acorns, gods or emperors and arms graduated in pounds and ounces; inscriptions record inspection by magistrates. Dates disagree by 250 years. [S: Museo Galileo Homo Faber; Topoi D-5-5]

## Medieval

- Thabit ibn Qurra (836-901, Harran and Baghdad; some references say 826), Kitab fi al-qarastun, the Book of the Steelyard: the founding text of the Arabic science of weights. He determines the counterweight for a heavy beam suspended off centre, which needs the law of the lever plus the beam's own moment, and shows that two equal loads balancing a third can be replaced by their sum at the midpoint. Latin as Liber karastonis by Gerard of Cremona (12th century), a direct source for Jordanus. [S: MacTutor; Muslim Heritage]
- Jordanus de Nemore (fl. c. 1220-1250). Elementa super demonstrationem ponderum: seven postulates, nine theorems. Postulates 4-5 define positional gravity: a weight "is heavier positionally, when, at a given position, its path of descent is less oblique." The law of the lever is proved by what is in effect work: "what suffices to lift a to D, would suffice to lift l through the distance LM," and since the two weights are equal neither is lifted. This is the first proof of the lever from the principle that one weight can raise another only by descending through a proportionally greater height. [P via Moody and Clagett 1952, p. 129; S: DSB]
- Jordanus gets the balance wrong (a displaced equal-arm balance does not return to horizontal) but the bent lever right. De ratione ponderis I.8: equal weights on unequal bent arms balance when their ends are "equidistant from the vertical line passing through the axis of support," which is the static moment, weight times horizontal distance, three centuries before it was named. [P via Moody and Clagett]
- Transmission: Apianus prints Jordanus in Nuremberg in 1533; Tartaglia reworks him in 1546; the full De ratione ponderis appears in Venice in 1565 from Tartaglia's papers. [S: DSB]

## Numbers for the illustrator and the physics stage

- Crowbar, 90 cm of handle and 10 cm of tip: advantage 9. A 60 kg person leaning their whole weight lifts about 540 kg, and the tip rises 1 cm for every 9 cm the handle drops. [derived]
- Seesaw: a 30 kg child 2 m from the pivot balances a 60 kg adult 1 m from it. [derived]
- Steelyard: a 1 kg counterpoise slid 50 cm along the arm balances 25 kg hung 2 cm from the pivot. That is why a market steelyard can weigh a pig with a weight the size of a fist. [derived]
- Forearm, roughly: the biceps inserts about 4 cm from the elbow and the hand is about 35 cm out, an advantage of about 0.11. Holding 5 kg in the hand takes about 44 kgf in the biceps, and the hand moves about nine times as far and as fast as the tendon. [derived from typical anatomical values, approximate]
- Galileo's five-to-one example: the force at the far end travels five times the distance the load rises, "il viaggio DI esser quintuplo allo spazio BG." [P]
- Moving the earth, for the fun of it: the earth weighs about 6 x 10^24 kg. With 60 kgf on the handle the arm ratio would be 10^23, and to lift the earth by 1 cm the hand would travel 10^21 m, about a hundred thousand light years, and Archimedes would have to bear down for longer than the universe has existed even at the speed of light. The boast is about proportion, not practicality. [derived]
- Class 3 in numbers: a fishing rod 3 m long held 30 cm from the butt gives an advantage of 0.1 and a tip speed ten times the hand's. [derived]

## Renaissance

- Leonardo da Vinci. Terms: lieva for the effort arm and contralieva for the load arm; "braccia reali" against "braccia potenziali," the potential arm being the perpendicular from the fulcrum to the line of force, which is how he handles bent levers and oblique cords, the same idea as Jordanus's bent lever and Benedetti's perpendiculars. Codex Atlanticus f. 176v-d: "The ratio of the weights which hold the arms of the balance parallel to the horizon is the same as that of the arms, but is an inverse one." Codex Arundel f. 32v copies Jordanus's bent-lever theorem into Italian and heads it "Sperimentata," tested. Codex Madrid I (BNE Ms. 8937, 192 folios, 1490s, rediscovered 1965) is his systematic study of machine elements. [S: Clagett, DSB "Leonardo da Vinci: Mechanics"; W for Madrid; specific Madrid folios U]
- Niccolo Tartaglia, Quesiti et inventioni diverse (Venice 1546), Book VIII "On the science of weights," a dialogue that restates Jordanus's propositions: velocity of descent proportional to weight, positional heaviness, force on a balance proportional to distance from the centre, the lever law in terms of positional heaviness, the inclined plane. Largely lifted from De ratione ponderis; Drake and Drabkin discuss the plagiarism charge. [S: Drake and Drabkin 1969; Omodeo and Renn 2019]
- Guidobaldo del Monte, Mechanicorum liber (Pesaro 1577; Italian by Pigafetta, 1581). Six treatises: De libra, De vecte, De trochlea, De axe in peritrochio, De cuneo, De cochlea. Only the centre of gravity is defined, after Commandino's Pappus and Archimedes. De vecte, proposition 1: the sustaining force is to the weight as the distance from fulcrum to weight is to the distance from fulcrum to force. Every later machine is reduced to the lever "by an ingenious method," proving each proposition first for a lever model and then transferring it. He attacks Jordanus and Tartaglia for "mistaking effects for causes": the swifter descent at the horizontal is a sign, not the reason, of greater heaviness. [S: Renn and Damerow 2010; Laird 2013]
- Guidobaldo's rigour cost him: the same insistence on equilibrium led him to reject Jordanus's correct inclined plane theorem in favour of Pappus's wrong one (see inclined-plane.md). He also held, against Jordanus and Tartaglia, that a balance displaced from horizontal stays where it is put, which is right. [S: DSB; Renn and Damerow]
- Giovanni Battista Benedetti, Diversarum speculationum (Turin 1585), "De mechanicis": the effect of a weight or force on a lever is measured "by means of perpendiculars drawn from the center of the balance to the line of inclination," which frees the lever from perpendicular forces. His chapter 5 treats a lever with the fulcrum at one end and the load between, a class 2 lever. Guidobaldo's marginal notes in his own copy call chapter 1 "taken entirely from our book on mechanics" and the method "empty and false." [S: Renn and Damerow 2012, The Equilibrium Controversy]
- Galileo, Le Mecaniche (Padua, c. 1593-1600). The lever chapter opens by calling the lever "one of the first principles, from which, as from a most fertile spring, many mechanical instruments derive," and states the rule: "qual proporzione ha la distanza BC alla distanza CD, tale abbia la forza posta in D, alla resistenza che fa il grave A," as BC is to CD, so is the force at D to the resistance of the heavy body A. He derives the lever from the balance, explains the steelyard (stadera, with its "romano" counterpoise), and then uses the lever to explain the windlass, the pulley and the screw. [P: Favaro edition, it.wikisource]
- Galileo on what the lever is for: its utility "is not that one might overcome and in a certain way deceive nature" but "the ability to move that heavy body all at once," which a small force could otherwise only do piece by piece. The distance the force travels is to the distance the load rises as the load is to the force: "il viaggio DI esser quintuplo allo spazio BG" in his five-to-one example. [P]
- Galileo, opening chapter: mechanics who believe they can "con poca forza muovere ed alzare grandissimi pesi" are "ingannandosi," deceiving themselves; nature's firm rule is "che niuna resistenza possa essere superata da forza, che di quella non sia più potente" (no resistance can be overcome by a force less powerful than it), and the speed of the force exceeds the speed of the load "tante volte ... quante esso peso è superiore alla forza." [P]

## The key insight and who had it

- Pseudo-Aristotle (c. 300 BC): the lever works because the far end moves faster on a bigger circle. Right answer, no proof.
- Archimedes (3rd century BC): the lever law proved from symmetry of equal weights at equal distances, the first mathematical theorem of mechanics. It rests on an unstated assumption about moments (Mach).
- Jordanus (13th century): the lever law proved from work, what one weight can lift the other must pay for by descending farther. The seed of virtual work.
- Galileo (c. 1600): machines cannot cheat nature; force times path is conserved; the lever is the model for every machine.

## Story beats

- A king asks for proof that a small force can move any weight, and a geometer, seated, hauls a loaded ship up the beach by hand. Plutarch's story, told for the lever, is in fact a story about pulleys, and the "place to stand" only survives in writers who lived five hundred to fourteen hundred years later. [P/S]
- Vitruvius's iron bar: a load "a multitude of hands cannot move," lifted by one man pressing down on the head of a lever, and the same chapter's steelyard, where a little acorn-shaped weight slid along the arm weighs a side of beef. [P]
- Leonardo copies a thirteenth-century theorem on the bent lever into his notebook and writes over it "Sperimentata," tested. [S]
- Guidobaldo, the leading mechanician of his day, reads Benedetti's new book and writes "empty and false" in the margin. [S]
- Galileo opens his mechanics by scolding the engineers who think a machine can trick nature: the force must run the course as many times as the load outweighs it. [P]

## Descendants

- Class 1: crowbar, claw hammer pulling a nail, scissors, pliers, seesaw, balance and steelyard, the oar (tholepin as fulcrum), a rowing boat, a hand pump handle.
- Class 2: wheelbarrow, nutcracker, bottle opener, car brake pedal, bellows, a door pushed near its edge.
- Class 3: tweezers, tongs, fishing rod, hoe, broom, swung hammer, baseball bat, the forearm, the jaw (modelled as class 3; class 2 for some rodent molar bites), an excavator's boom and dipper, where the hydraulic cylinder sits between the pivot and the bucket and trades cylinder force for reach and speed. [W/S; excavator by definition, U for a source]
- Compound: nail clippers, piano key and action, the ossicles of the middle ear, the joints of a folding rule. [W]
- Balance scales of every kind, from the Egyptian equal-arm balance to the Roman steelyard to the laboratory beam balance, are levers whose only job is to be exact.

## Misconceptions to avoid

- "Every lever gives a mechanical advantage." Class 3 levers never do; they amplify motion.
- "Machines create force or energy." Galileo's opening chapter is written against this; the lever multiplies force by dividing distance.
- "A balance is level when the weights are equal." Only at equal distances; students routinely miss the distance term (Tosa and Uchida 2018). [S]
- "A longer lever is always better." Longer costs travel and time, the bar's own weight, and bending; pseudo-Aristotle already notes that the lever's weight is added to the load.
- "Archimedes said 'give me a lever.'" He is reported to have asked for a place to stand; the lever word appears only in Tzetzes in the 12th century, and the demonstration Plutarch describes used pulleys.
- "The claw hammer is a class 1 lever." Pulling a nail, yes; swung, it is class 3. The class is about use, not the object.
