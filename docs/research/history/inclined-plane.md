# The inclined plane

The ramp is the oldest of the six machines in use and the youngest in theory. Humans dragged stone up slopes two and a half thousand years before anyone wrote down the rule, and the rule itself defeated the best Greek mechanicians. It was finally proved by a medieval cleric nobody can identify, forgotten, rediscovered by a Flemish bookkeeper with a wreath of beads, and put on a sound footing by Galileo, who then showed that the screw is the same machine rolled up.

Reliability tags used below: [P] primary source seen in text or translation, [S] scholarly secondary source, [W] Wikipedia-level, [U] unverified or recalled and to be checked before use.

## Physics in plain terms

- A load of weight W raised through a height h along a ramp of length L needs, in the ideal frictionless case, only the force F = W h / L = W sin(theta), where theta is the slope angle. [textbook]
- Ideal mechanical advantage: MA = L / h = 1 / sin(theta). A 1-in-12 ramp lets one push with roughly one twelfth of the weight. [textbook]
- The trade is force for distance: the load rises h but the pusher walks L. Work in equals work out: F L = W h. The ramp lowers the force, never the work. [textbook; Galileo's statement below]
- The normal force is N = W cos(theta); friction adds mu N to the push. With a friction angle phi = arctan(mu), the real advantage pushing uphill is cos(phi) / sin(theta + phi). [textbook]
- Angle of repose: a block stays put when tan(theta) is less than mu, so theta = phi is the steepest slope that holds. Euler proved in 1750 that the tangent of the angle of repose equals the coefficient of friction; Leonardo had measured sliding friction around 1493 but left it unpublished, Amontons restated the laws in 1699 and Coulomb refined them in 1785. [W, citing Meyer 2002 and Armstrong-Helouvry 1991]
- Efficiency falls as the ramp gets shallower, because the path gets longer and friction acts over all of it, while the normal force hardly changes. A ramp can only ever approach the ideal W h from above.
- The wedge is an inclined plane driven under a load; the screw is an inclined plane wound round a cylinder. Galileo's chapter on the screw makes both reductions explicit (see Renaissance below). [P]

## Why it is the odd one out

- It has no moving part and occurs in nature as any hillside, so the Greeks, who defined a machine by its parts, did not count it. Heron's Mechanics (1st century AD) lists five "powers": wheel and axle, lever, pulley, wedge, screw. Pappus (c. AD 320) repeats the five. [S: Heath, History of Greek Mathematics II; Drachmann]
- The objection outlived the Renaissance. As late as 1826 Karl von Langsdorf argued the plane "is no more a machine than is the slope of a mountain." [W, citing Festa and Roux 2008]
- It entered the list only after its law was proved (Jordanus in the 13th century, Stevin in 1586, Galileo around 1600), and only became the standard sixth member in the 18th-century textbooks of the "mechanical powers." The 1842 Encyclopaedia Britannica says the elementary machines "have been generally reckoned six in number": lever, wheel and axle, pulley, inclined plane, wedge, screw. [P: EB 7th ed., "Mechanics"]

## Earliest evidence

- Hatnub, Egypt (published 2018). A joint mission of the Institut francais d'archeologie orientale and the University of Liverpool (Yannis Gourdon and Roland Enmarch) found a rock-cut haulage ramp in the Egyptian alabaster quarry at Hatnub: a central incline flanked by two rock-cut staircases lined with rows of postholes. Blocks on sledges were hauled by ropes turned around the wooden posts, which acted as force multipliers. Inscriptions of Khufu date the quarry work to his reign, c. 2560 BC. Press reports give the slope as 20 percent or steeper, far steeper than a century of reconstructions of Old Kingdom ramps had assumed. Enmarch is explicit that using this at Giza is inference, not proof. [S: Enmarch, The Conversation, 8 Nov 2018; archaeology.org news, Oct 2018]
- The Great Pyramid ramps. No construction ramp survives at Giza. A straight external ramp at a workable 7 to 8 percent would run about 1.5 km and overrun the plateau; zigzag and spiral ramps hide the corners and sightlines needed to keep the edges true; no trace of a vast spiral has been found. Jean-Pierre Houdin's internal spiral ramp (publicised 2007 with Dassault Systemes) remains unproven and was called "far-fetched" by David Jeffreys of UCL and "potentially promising" by Zahi Hawass. Treat every Giza ramp as conjecture. [W; Smithsonian]
- Nineveh, c. 700 BC. Reliefs from Sennacherib's palace (British Museum, found by Layard in 1849) show teams of prisoners hauling a winged bull colossus on a sledge with ropes, while men work a long lever wedged under the sledge whenever it sticks and Sennacherib watches from his chariot under a parasol. The sledge is the usual reading of the slab; that it climbs an earthen ramp to the palace terrace is Layard's interpretation. [S: British Museum; Layard]
- Lachish, 701 BC. The Assyrian siege ramp of stone and earth against the south-west corner of Lachish is the oldest siege ramp known and the only one found in the ancient Near East. David Ussishkin's excavations (1973-1994) also found, in 1983, a Judean counter-ramp built inside the wall to keep the defenders above the attackers. The siege is carved on the Lachish reliefs from Nineveh, now in the British Museum. [W; Biblical Archaeology Review]
- Masada, AD 73 (some argue 74). The Tenth Legion under Flavius Silva raised a 114 m assault ramp against the western cliff. Dan Gill showed in Nature (1993) that the ramp is mostly a natural bedrock spur with Roman fill on top; a battering ram was hauled up it and the wall breached. [S: Gill, Nature 364, 1993]
- The Diolkos at Corinth, built in the late 7th or early 6th century BC, was a paved, grooved trackway 6 to 8 km long that carried ship cargoes and sometimes hulls over the isthmus ridge, with an average grade near 1 in 70 and stretches around 1 in 16. It is an inclined plane as civil engineering. [W]
- Stonehenge and the ziggurats: the earthen ramps in every reconstruction are inferred, not excavated. Say "probably" on the placard. [W]

## Antiquity: the problem nobody could solve

- Pseudo-Aristotle, Mechanical Problems (c. 300 BC, 35 problems): the wedge is problem 17 and the pulley 18, but no problem treats a weight resting on a slope. William Whewell noted in 1858 that the inclined plane "is not treated of in the Mechanical Problems." [P: Loeb text; S: Whewell]
- Heron, Mechanics I.23, tries a cylinder resting on a slope. He cuts the cylinder by a vertical plane through its line of contact, observes that the uphill part is smaller than the downhill part, and concludes that the force needed to hold it equals the weight of the excess. Cohen and Drabkin's translation: "We need, therefore, a force equivalent to this difference to preserve equilibrium." The answer is wrong; the excess segment does not vary as sin(theta). [P via Cohen and Drabkin 1958; S: Drachmann 1963]
- Pappus, Collection VIII, proposition 9 (c. AD 320): "Given a weight which can be drawn along a plane parallel to the horizon by a given force, and a plane inclined to the horizon at a given angle, to find the force required to draw the weight upwards on the inclined plane." He rests a sphere on the slope, imagines a horizontal balance through its centre with the fulcrum vertically above the contact point, and finds the balancing weight; then he assumes a force is needed to move the weight even on level ground and adds the two. Worked example: a 200-talent sphere on a 60-degree slope, 40 men on the level, 300 men on the slope. Heath's verdict: "the first or only attempt in ancient times to investigate motion on an inclined plane, and as such it is curious, though of no value." The answer goes to infinity at 90 degrees. [P via Heath II, pp. 433-434; S: Laird 2013; Bertoloni Meli 2006]
- Pappus's wrong answer was printed in Commandino's Latin translation (Pesaro 1588), adopted by Guidobaldo, and refuted only by Galileo. It stood for almost thirteen centuries.
- Nothing in the Arabic tradition treats the slope: Thabit ibn Qurra's Book of the Steelyard is about the lever, and al-Khazini's Book of the Balance of Wisdom (1121) is about balances. [S: absence claim, treat as [U] until checked]

## Medieval: Jordanus gets it right

- Jordanus de Nemore worked in the first half of the 13th century; nothing else is known about him. His works were already listed in Richard de Fournival's library catalogue (compiled 1246-1260). [S: Dictionary of Scientific Biography, "Jordanus"]
- De ratione ponderis (45 propositions), attributed to him with some doubt, is "probably the most significant of all medieval statical treatises" (DSB). Moody and Clagett edited and translated it in The Medieval Science of Weights (Madison 1952), where the inclined plane theorem is R1.10. [S]
- Proposition 10: "If two weights descend along diversely inclined planes, then, if the inclinations are directly proportional to the weights, they will be of equal force in descending." Inclination is measured by the vertical drop per unit of path, which is the sine of the angle. In modern terms this is F = W sin(theta). [P via Moody and Clagett; S: DSB]
- The proof is a thought experiment on displacement: if one weight could pull the other up its slope, then it could equally raise an equal weight on an equal slope through an equal height, which is impossible because equal weights on equal slopes are of equal force. This is the ancestor of the principle of virtual work. [S: DSB; Festa and Roux 2008]
- The tool was "positional gravity" (gravitas secundum situm): a weight is heavier in position when its path of descent is less oblique. It gives correct results on straight paths and wrong ones on circular arcs. [S: DSB]
- Jordanus compared two slopes to each other rather than a slope to the horizontal. That is exactly the trap Pappus fell into and Jordanus avoided.
- Transmission: printed by Petrus Apianus (Nuremberg 1533); propositions reproduced in Tartaglia's Quesiti et inventioni diverse (Venice 1546), Book VIII; the full De ratione ponderis printed posthumously from Tartaglia's papers by Curtius Troianus as Jordani opusculum de ponderositate (Venice 1565). [S: DSB; Laird 2013]

## Numbers for the illustrator and the physics stage

- A 1-in-12 ramp (the ADA limit) rises 4.8 degrees; the ideal push is W/12. On a sled with a friction coefficient of 0.3 the real push is W (sin 4.8 + 0.3 cos 4.8), about 0.38 W, an efficiency of 22 percent. On wheels with rolling resistance 0.02 it is about 0.10 W, efficiency about 80 percent. The ramp's advantage is only as good as the friction under the load. [derived]
- Hatnub's 20 percent slope is 11.3 degrees. A 1000 kg block on a sled with friction 0.3 needs a pull of about 490 kgf; with the rope turned round a post the haulers can rest without losing ground, which is what the postholes are for. [derived from the excavators' description; the friction value is an assumption]
- Pappus's worked example, checked: a 200-talent sphere on a 60-degree slope. His balance gives 1300 talents for the counterweight and, with 40 men needed on the level, 300 men on the slope. The true ideal holding force is 200 sin 60, about 173 talents, less than the weight itself; his method overestimates by a factor of about 7.5 and goes to infinity at 90 degrees, where the true answer is simply the weight. [derived from Heath's paraphrase]
- Stevin's wreath: fourteen beads, four on a slope of length 2 and two on a slope of length 1, both slopes rising the same height. Pull along the long slope is 4 units spread over length 2, pull along the short is 2 units over length 1; they balance, so force on a slope is as height to length. [P]
- Galileo's law of odd numbers: in equal ticks a ball rolling from rest covers 1, 3, 5, 7 units, on any incline. The acceleration along the slope is g sin(theta) reduced for rolling (5/7 of that for a solid ball). On a 2 m plank at 10 degrees a solid ball takes about 1.8 s; at 5 degrees about 2.6 s. Slow enough to time with a water clock, which was the point. [derived]
- A rule of thumb for the caption: halve the slope and you halve the force but double the road.

## Renaissance

- Leonardo da Vinci states the problem in the Codex Arundel (ff. 205, 213-215 per the Source Library entry, folio numbers [U]) but does not solve it. [S: Festa and Roux abstract]
- Girolamo Cardano gives a wrong rule, force proportional to the angle of slope, so half the weight at 45 degrees. Sources disagree whether it is in De subtilitate (1550) or Opus novum de proportionibus (1570); the rule is the same. [W/S]
- Guidobaldo del Monte, Mechanicorum liber (Pesaro 1577; Italian by Filippo Pigafetta, Venice 1581), treats the balance and five machines and has no chapter on the plane. In the wedge and screw chapters he cites Pappus's theorem without printing it; Pigafetta added it in the translation. Guidobaldo's own unpublished attempt in the Meditatiunculae reduces the plane to a lever and yields absurd results for steep slopes. The DSB's verdict: his Archimedean rigour "led him to reject Jordanus' correct inclined-plane theorem in favor of an erroneous explanation by Pappus." [S: Laird 2013; DSB]
- Michael Varro, De motu tractatus (Geneva 1584), reached a correct solution independently. [W, citing Festa and Roux]
- Simon Stevin, De Beghinselen der Weeghconst (Leiden 1586), Book I, proposition 19. Take a triangular prism with one sloping face twice as long as the other. Hang over it a closed necklace of fourteen equal spheres on a string, the clootcrans or "wreath of spheres": four rest on the long slope, two on the short, eight hang below. The hanging part is symmetric and pulls equally both ways. If the four did not balance the two, the wreath would turn for ever of its own accord, "which is absurd." Therefore weights on two slopes balance when they are as the lengths of the slopes, and a weight on a slope is held by a force as the height is to the length. [P: Stevin, via Dijksterhuis, Principal Works I, 1955; S: Linda Hall Library]
- Beneath the figure Stevin wrote "Wonder en is gheen wonder" (the "en ... gheen" is an emphatic Dutch double negative): "wonder is no wonder," or in Devreese and Vanden Berghe's rendering "magic is no magic." He used the wreath and motto as the title-page vignette of all his 1586 treatises. [P for the Dutch; S for the title page]
- Dijksterhuis observed that Stevin had "intuitively made use of the principle of conservation of energy" long before anyone stated it; the argument is not fully tight (why must the necklace not creep?), but it is the first published correct proof and the one that put the plane on the list. [S: Dijksterhuis 1955]
- Galileo, De motu (Pisa, c. 1590, unpublished), chapter on "the ratio of the speeds of bodies moving along various inclined planes": he isolates the vertical displacement with a bent lever and gets the static ratio right, then wrongly equates the ratio of forces to the ratio of speeds and admits the numbers do not match experiment. [W; S: Festa and Roux]
- Galileo, Le Mecaniche (Padua, c. 1593-1600, printed in French by Mersenne in 1634 and in Italian in 1649), chapter "Della vite": "sopra il piano elevato la forza al peso avere la medesima proporzione, che la perpendicolare dal termine del piano tirata all'orizonte, alla lunghezza d'esso piano" (on the raised plane the force is to the weight as the perpendicular from the top of the plane down to the horizon is to the length of the plane). He proves it with a weight carried on a circle whose moment falls as it descends, the bent lever again. [P: Favaro edition on it.wikisource]
- Galileo on Pappus, same chapter: Pappus "non ha toccato lo scopo, e si e abbagliato nell'assunto che lui fa, dove suppone, il peso dover esser mosso nel piano orizontale da una forza data: il che e falso" (missed the mark, dazzled by his assumption that a weight must be moved on the horizontal by a given force: which is false). A body on a level plane is indifferent to motion and any force at all moves it. [P]
- Then the screw: "la forma ed essenza primaria della vite non e altro che un simil triangolo ACB ... lo circondo ed avvolse intorno al cilindro" (the primary form and essence of the screw is nothing but such a triangle ... wound around the cylinder). The advantage of a screw is thread length to rise, the same ratio as length to height on a ramp. [P]
- Two New Sciences (Leiden 1638), Third Day: the ramp becomes an instrument for slowing gravity. A bronze ball rolls down a parchment-lined groove in a twelve-cubit beam; time is measured by weighing the water that runs from a tank through a thin pipe into a glass during the descent. "Repeated a full hundred times," the distances were "as the squares of the times, and this was true for all inclinations of the plane," to within "one-tenth of a pulse-beat." [P: Crew and de Salvio translation]
- Drake dated Galileo's working notes on the inclined plane (BNCF Ms. Gal. 72, f. 107v) to 1604; the 16 October 1604 letter to Sarpi states the times-squared law from a wrong premise. [S: Drake; letter date [U]]

## The key insight and who had it

- The correct law belongs to Jordanus (13th century), whose proof is a virtual-displacement argument. It was in print from 1533 and 1546 but disbelieved by the Archimedean school (Guidobaldo). [S]
- The first published proof that convinced everyone belongs to Stevin (1586), and its engine is the impossibility of perpetual motion, an energy argument before "energy." [S]
- The first proof that explains why, and links the plane to every other machine through the bent lever and the trade of force for distance, belongs to Galileo (c. 1600), who also exposed Pappus's error. [P]
- The three proofs are three faces of one idea: virtual work. Lagrange made it the foundation of all statics in 1788. See README.md.

## Story beats

- Enmarch went to Hatnub to record quarrymen's inscriptions and found, cut into the rock, a ramp twice as steep as textbooks allowed, with staircases and postholes for the ropes. [S]
- Pappus said 300 men for a 200-talent sphere on a 60-degree slope. The number stood in the best mathematics of Alexandria, was printed in Pesaro in 1588, and was still the leading mechanician's preferred answer, until Galileo wrote "il che e falso." [S/P]
- Stevin's necklace of fourteen beads is a proof by refusing to believe in a perpetual motion machine, drawn so plainly that he made it his emblem. The often-told story that it is carved on his gravestone is a legend; no grave of Stevin is known. [S; Feynman's admiring remark [U]]
- Galileo timed his rolling ball by weighing water, a hundred times over, to a tenth of a heartbeat. [P]

## Descendants

- Roads, switchbacks and zigzag ramps: the same trade of distance for grade. Stairs are a ramp cut into steps. Loading ramps and wheelchair ramps: the US ADA maximum running slope is 1:12. [W]
- Funiculars: Lyon opened the first in 1862 (one popular source says 1860); the Reisszug at Hohensalzburg is documented from 1515 and may be the oldest surviving railway, though whether it ever had a counterweight is debated. [W]
- The aircraft-carrier ski jump: proposed by Lt Cdr Doug Taylor RN in 1973, trialled at RAE Bedford in 1977, built at 7 degrees on HMS Invincible (first sea take-off 30 October 1980) and at 12 degrees on Ark Royal. The ramp turns deck speed into a climbing arc that buys time to accelerate. [W]
- Knife, axe, chisel and plough are the plane in motion (wedge); bolt, jack and press are the plane wound up (screw).

## Misconceptions to avoid

- "The ramp reduces the work." It reduces the force; the work W h is the same and friction adds to it.
- "A shallower ramp is always easier." Easier per step, but the path is longer and friction acts along all of it; the shallow ramp wastes more energy.
- "The Greeks did not know the ramp." They built with it constantly; they did not count it as a machine and could not derive its law.
- "Stevin invented the law." Jordanus had it three centuries earlier; Stevin's proof is the first that persuaded, and Galileo's is the first that explained.
- "Heron listed six machines." Five. The plane is the late arrival.
- Do not present any Giza ramp geometry as fact. Hatnub is the only excavated Old Kingdom haulage ramp with a documented slope.
